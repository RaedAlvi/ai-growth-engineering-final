Section A: The 12 most likely MCQs

1. CVM (Customer Value Management) is primarily about:

  1. Acquiring as many new customers as possible
  2. Increasing the lifetime value of existing customers
  3. Cutting support costs
  4. Maximizing ad channels

Answer: B. CVM maximizes profitability by raising the CLV of customers you already have, not just acquisition.

2. A company finds CAC is now greater than CLV. The course calls this:

  1. A balanced model
  2. The do-nothing scenario
  3. Start-up Demise
  4. The mobile mind shift

Answer: C. Spending more to acquire a customer than they are worth over their lifetime is "Start-up Demise". Healthy is CAC < CLV.

3. "GenAI does not replace ML or Applied AI, it expands the toolbox" supports which idea?

  1. Agentic AI is always best
  2. AI is a stack of fit-for-purpose layers, not one tool
  3. Expert systems are obsolete
  4. Deep Learning replaces ML

Answer: B. AI is a stack; each paradigm fits a different decision. This is the most reused exam line.

4. Which AI layer is "The Predictor", used for credit scoring, churn, and NBA?

  1. Expert Systems
  2. Machine Learning
  3. Generative AI
  4. Agentic AI

Answer: B. Machine Learning predicts a probability ("if X happened, Y has a probability"). Its risk is degrading as data changes.

5. A directional (one-tailed) alternative hypothesis means:

  1. Any change counts, up or down
  2. Only an increase (uplift) counts
  3. The null is auto-rejected
  4. Significance is impossible

Answer: B. Directional asks "did it increase?"; only uplift counts. Non-directional (two-tailed) counts up or down.

6. MVP/Basic versus Augmented product. The Augmented product is:

  1. The minimum offering for the core benefit
  2. Features that go beyond the customer's minimum expectations
  3. The cheapest version
  4. A prototype with bugs

Answer: B. MVP/Basic delivers the core benefit at minimum; Augmented adds features beyond minimum expectations.

7. Selling a product below cost to pull in customers and profit on follow-on sales is:

  1. Penetration pricing
  2. Economy pricing
  3. Loss leader strategy
  4. Premium pricing

Answer: C. Loss leader (Amazon Kindle, Gillette razors). Penetration is a low launch price you later raise.

8. For digital products with near-zero marginal cost, the "holy grail" pricing is:

  1. Cost-plus
  2. Competitive
  3. Value-based
  4. Dynamic

Answer: C. Value-based pricing captures perceived value; ideal where marginal cost is near zero. Cost-plus and dynamic are "less relevant" for digital.

9. Stickiness ratio is defined as:

  1. MAU divided by DAU
  2. DAU divided by MAU
  3. Churn divided by retention
  4. ARPU divided by CAC

Answer: B. Stickiness = DAU/MAU, how often monthly users return on a daily basis.

10. In Next Best Action, the golden principle is right offer, right customer, right time, and:

  1. Right price
  2. Right channel
  3. Right region
  4. Right product

Answer: B. "Right offer to the right customer at the right time through the right channel."

11. In the Base Exclusion Funnel, "slide back" rescues customers who:

  1. Are flagged as fraud
  2. Have the lowest scores
  3. Sit in the top 3 model deciles despite being in a score tail
  4. Never transacted

Answer: C. Slide-back rescues high-propensity customers (top 3 deciles) who were excluded by the score limits.

12. The Target vs Control experiment isolates the:

  1. Modelling effect
  2. Communication effect
  3. Best message
  4. Price elasticity

Answer: B. Target (gets the message) vs Control (gets none) measures whether the communication itself changed behavior.

Section B: The 6 most likely long answers

B1. Compare Applied AI, Generative AI, and Agentic AI. Define each, give an example decision for each, and state how they relate. (This is the "two types of AI" sessional question, expanded.)

Applied AI is the predictive and assistive group: Expert Systems (rules, certainty, compliance), Machine Learning (probabilities, churn and credit scoring), and Deep Learning (pattern discovery, fraud and images). Generative AI creates new content from intent (summaries, conversation), with hallucination risk. Agentic AI plans and acts autonomously across multi-step workflows, with the risk of automating mistakes fast, so it needs guardrails. The relationship: AI is a stack, fit-for-purpose, and GenAI expands the toolbox rather than replacing ML or Applied AI. See AI and Growth Engineering for the full table and worked scenarios.

B2. Differentiate MVP/Basic versus Augmented product, with a fintech example. Where does the core benefit sit? (The "MVP vs" sessional question.)

The core benefit sits at the center (the fundamental reason the customer uses the product, for example "move money safely"). MVP/Basic is the minimum offering that delivers that core benefit. Augmented adds features beyond minimum expectations (rewards, analytics, advisory). Fintech example: a wallet MVP is send, receive, and check balance; the augmented wallet adds bill split, cashback, budgeting, and a credit score. See Digital Products.

B3. List and explain the 8-step product design process in order.

1 Idea Generation, 2 Idea Screening, 3 Concept Development and Testing, 4 Marketing Strategy and Business Analysis, 5 Product Prototype Development, 6 Iterative Testing, 7 Commercialization, 8 Breakeven and projections. Full explanation in Digital Products.

B4. Outline the 9 pricing strategies and explain why competitive pricing is risky for digital products while value-based is the holy grail.

Cost-plus, Competitive, Penetration, Dynamic, Price Discrimination, Economy, Premium, Loss Leader, Value-based. Competitive pricing drags digital players into a "red ocean" of low margins; better to compete on uniqueness. Value-based captures perceived value and suits near-zero marginal cost. See Pricing.

B5. Explain the Next Best Action four-tier framework and its golden principle.

Understand the customer (strategic plus tactical segmentation, propensity scores), Decide the most relevant offer (micro-segments, three prioritized offers, dormancy 0 to 150 days), Place and pitch on channel (inbound and outbound), and Adaptive learning and self-healing (decline scores fed back). Principle: right offer, right customer, right time, right channel. See Next Best Action.

B6. Explain null versus alternative hypothesis and directional versus non-directional tests, and link them to the three CVM experiments.

Null = the communication does not change the response rate; Alternative = it does. Directional (one-tailed) counts only uplift; non-directional (two-tailed) counts any change. You reject the null only with statistical significance. The experiments produce the data: Target vs Control (communication effect), A/B (best message), Model Performance (modelling effect). See AI and Growth Engineering.

Attack guide

Numeric Labs Playbook how to tackle the Excel questions

Read this if the numbers scare you. The three Excel labs (the Pricing Game, the Base Exclusion Funnel, and the Design-Your-Own-Product model) look intimidating, but each one is really just a couple of formulas applied in order. Each card below is an attack plan: how to recognize the question, the exact steps, the formula, a worked example, and the trap to dodge, with a rough time budget. The deep teaching is in the topic sections; this is the card you run in the exam.

The routine for any numeric question

  1. Read it twice and underline the target. What single number (or list) are they actually asking for?
  2. Write the formula first, before any numbers. The formula on the page earns marks even if the arithmetic slips later.
  3. Substitute the given numbers into the formula, showing the substitution.
  4. Compute carefully. Watch decimals, percentages (13.5% is 0.135), and per year vs per month (divide by 12).
  5. Sanity-check the size, then leave your working visible. Partial credit lives in the steps, never write a bare final number.
01
The Pricing Game
Full teaching: Pricing
about 10 to 12 min
The sheet
Recognize

A table of customer segments (A, B, C, D) with columns for number of customers, a subscription amount, a pricing rate, and a revenue column, plus a task like "increase total revenue by 20%" and a constraint like "only two segments may change".

Attack
  1. Compute the current total by adding Customers x SubAmount x Pricing for every segment. This is your baseline.
  2. Compute the target. For +20%, target = current x 1.20. Note the gap to close.
  3. Spot the lever: the segment with the biggest Customers x SubAmount moves the most money (here, segment A).
  4. Raise the pricing rate on your chosen segments (respect the "max two" rule). New revenue = Customers x (cut subscription if orange, else normal) x new pricing.
  5. Add the new total and adjust until it reaches or just passes the target.
Formula
Segment revenue = Customers x Subscription Amount x Pricing rate. Total = the sum across segments. An "orange" segment has its subscription amount cut by 5% (multiply it by 0.95).
Worked
Lift 500,000 to 600,000 (+20%), only 2 segments may change.

Make A and B "orange" (5% subscription cut), raise their prices:
  A: 100 x (25,000 x 0.95 = 23,750) x 0.16  = 380,000
  B:  80 x (20,000 x 0.95 = 19,000) x 0.102 = 155,040
  C (unchanged): 70 x 10,000 x 0.075 = 52,500
  D (unchanged): 50 x  5,000 x 0.05  = 12,500
                                TOTAL = 600,040   ->  +20.0%
Trap
Making a segment orange cuts its subscription amount by 5%, which lowers revenue. So the price increase on an orange segment must more than cancel out that 5% loss. Never make a segment orange unless you raise its price enough to come out ahead.
02
The Base Exclusion Funnel
Full teaching: Base Exclusion Funnel
about 12 to 15 min
The sheet
Recognize

Talk of a score (roughly 0 to 400), a bell curve or normal distribution, mu and sigma, "retain 95%", deciles, "slide back", a fraud or gaming flag, and a funnel summary with Head / Tail / Retained / Excluded.

Idea
  • mu is the average score; sigma is how spread out the scores are.
  • A bell curve says about 95% of values sit within mu plus or minus 2 sigma, so "keep the middle 95%" means cut the two extreme tails.
  • Four flags per customer: Qualify (score inside the limits), Slide back (top 3 model deciles, so rescue), Retained (Qualify OR Slide back), Final (Retained AND not a fraudster).
Attack
  1. Get mu and sigma (given, or compute the mean and standard deviation).
  2. Build the band: Lower = mu minus 2 sigma, Upper = mu plus 2 sigma.
  3. Sanity-check the band against the real min and max. If it is wider than the data range, mu plus or minus 2 sigma excludes nobody, so use the practical limits the question gives (here near 20 and 350).
  4. Apply the four flags in order: Qualify, then Slide back, then Retained, then Final.
  5. Read the funnel left to right: Total, then after the score trim, then after slide-back (retained goes UP), then after fraud removal (retained goes DOWN).
Worked
This dataset: mu = 170.85, sigma = 103.12, scores 1 to 368.
Band = 170.85 +/- (2 x 103.12) = [-35.4, 377.1]   wider than 1..368, cuts NOBODY.
So the lab uses practical limits: Lower = 20, Upper = 350.

Classify 3 customers (Lower 20, Upper 350, top deciles = 1,2,3):
  X  score 360, decile 2, fraud 0 -> Qualify? 360>350 NO. Slide back? decile 2 YES
                                  -> Retained YES, clean -> FINAL = KEEP (rescued)
  Y  score 12,  decile 7, fraud 0 -> Qualify? 12<20 NO. Slide back? NO
                                  -> Retained NO -> EXCLUDED
  Z  score 300, decile 1, fraud 1 -> Qualify? 20<300<350 YES -> Retained YES
                                  -> fraud=1 -> FINAL = REMOVED (fraud beats all)

Whole base: Retained 90.42% -> 93.31% (slide-back) -> 89.11% (after fraud).
Trap
Do not blindly trust mu plus or minus 2 sigma, check it against the actual min and max first. Keep the direction straight: slide-back raises retention (it rescues high-value customers), fraud removal lowers it, and fraud is removed last so a fraudster never sneaks through on a good score. (Retained is 90.42% not 95% because the scores are lopsided, so the tails are not symmetric.)

The Design-Your-Own-Product Model
Full teaching: Design Your Own Product
about 8 to 10 min
The sheet
Recognize

An investment product for daily wage earners, with a cohort size, an invested amount, "per subscriber" figures, an organization share %, a customer monthly profit %, platform charges, and Basic / Intermediate / Advance variants.

Formula
Work the chain top to bottom: (1) Invested per subscriber = Invested Amount divided by Cohort size. (2) Org monthly share per sub = (org %) x (per-sub invested) divided by 12. (3) Customer monthly profit per sub = (profit %) x (per-sub invested) divided by 12. (4) Other-legs revenue per sub = (other-legs %) x (per-sub invested). (5) After the 15% platform charge, keep 85% (x 0.85). (6) What the customer earns = other legs after charge PLUS customer monthly profit.
Attack
  1. Find which number they want and locate its line in the chain above.
  2. Work down the chain only as far as you need; each line feeds the next.
  3. Convert annual rates to monthly by dividing by 12 whenever a monthly figure is asked.
Worked
Basic cohort: 34,493 subscribers, Year 1 invested = 56,972,810.

Invested per subscriber = 56,972,810 / 34,493 = 1,651.72
Org monthly share at 13.5%  = 0.135 x 1,651.72 / 12 = 18.58 per sub
Customer monthly profit 9%  = 0.09  x 1,651.72 / 12 = 12.39 per sub
Trap
Two classics: forgetting to divide annual rates by 12 for a monthly figure, and confusing what the customer earns with what the organization earns (different formulas, so re-read which is asked). Variant rule: Basic to Intermediate to Advance, the customer count roughly halves while the margin split shifts.

AI & Growth Engineering WEEK 6

What this week is really about

The lecture title is "Bridging the Gap" and the opening line reframes the whole week as moving "from minding the gap to closing the gap." The "gap" is the distance between the value a customer could give a business and the value the business is actually capturing today. Growth Engineering is the disciplined, data-driven, experiment-driven practice of closing that gap. This week ties together three big ideas:

  • CVM (Customer Value Management) as the operating philosophy for growth.
  • Experimentation and hypothesis testing as the way we prove growth actions actually work.
  • The AI Paradigms Stack as the toolbox that powers modern, contextual, real-time growth (with the hard warning that bad data ruins all of it: GIGO).

Read the four pillars below in order. Everything connects: CVM tells you what to do, segmentation tells you to whom, experiments tell you whether it worked, and the AI stack tells you with which tool.

1. CVM: Customer Value Management

Definition and the core mindset shift

The lecture states it plainly: "When we talk about Growth, we talk about CVM." CVM is the lens through which the entire week views growth.

  • CLV (Customer Lifetime Value) first, because it sits at the center of the definition. The companion metrics sheet defines it as "predicts the total revenue a business can expect from a single customer account throughout their relationship." It is the total value a customer brings over their entire time with you, not just one purchase. (The metrics sheet writes it CLTV; the lecture writes CLV. Same thing.)
  • CVM (Customer Value Management) is then "a strategic, data-driven operating approach that maximizes a business's profitability by focusing on increasing the lifetime value (CLV) of existing customers rather than just acquiring new ones."

The key mindset shift for the exam: CVM is about growing the value of customers you already have, not only chasing new ones. Acquisition is expensive; deepening existing relationships is where durable profit lives.

Key term. CVM (Customer Value Management). A strategic, data-driven operating approach that maximizes a business's profitability by focusing on increasing the lifetime value (CLV) of existing customers rather than just acquiring new ones.
Key term. CLV (Customer Lifetime Value). The total revenue a business can expect from a single customer across the whole relationship. CVM exists to push this number up.

The four questions CVM asks about every customer

The lecture says CVM is about understanding "not just who someone is but where they are, what they are doing, and what they are likely to do next." Memorize these four as a set:

  • WHO someone is (identity, demographics).
  • WHERE they are (location, context, channel, device).
  • WHAT they are doing (current behavior, current activity).
  • WHAT they are likely to do NEXT (prediction, propensity, the next-best action).

It is then "about combining the right information about a customer and the context to deliver the right services and communication at the precise moment it offers the most value." That phrase, "the right thing at the right moment," is the heart of contextual marketing and it reappears in the Data Mix section as acting "in the moment."

The 5 Market Imperatives

The lecture says organizations must use modern CVM frameworks to "preserve value around 5 market imperatives." Learn all five by name. A simple memory hook: Mobile, Big-data, CX, Disruptor, AI.

  • Embrace the mobile mind shift. Customers expect to do anything, anywhere, instantly, on their phone. Design for that expectation.
  • Turn big data into business insights. Raw data is worthless; the imperative is converting volume into decisions.
  • Transform the customer experience (CX). Compete on experience, not just product.
  • Become a digital disruptor. Use digital channels and models to change the game rather than defend the old one.
  • Embrace, welcome, and practice AI. AI is treated as a market imperative, not a nice-to-have. This is the bridge into the AI stack later in the week.
Exam trap. Do not confuse the "four questions" (who / where / what they do / what next) with the "5 market imperatives" (mobile mind shift, big data to insights, transform CX, digital disruptor, embrace AI). They are two different lists from two different parts of the CVM slide. Examiners love to mix items between the two.

2. The CAC vs CLV Balance

When to keep investing, and when you are doomed

The lecture's rule: "A well-balanced business model means the organization continues investing as long as customer acquisition cost stays less than the Customer Lifetime Value (while still figuring out the means to keep the delta in check)."

  • CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) is defined in the metrics sheet as "the total cost involved in acquiring a new customer." It is what you spend to win one customer (ads, sales, onboarding, incentives).
  • The healthy condition is CAC < CLV. If a customer is worth more over their lifetime than it cost to acquire them, every new customer adds value, so you keep investing.
  • The "delta" is the gap between CLV and CAC (CLV minus CAC). You want this delta positive and you must "keep it in check," meaning watch it so it does not silently shrink.
  • The danger zone is CAC > CLV. The lecture labels this state bluntly: "Company is spending more to acquire new customers than their lifetime value, then arrow, Start-up Demise!" Spending more to win a customer than they will ever return is a guaranteed slow death.
  • k = the tenure of the customer with the product/organization. Tenure means how long the customer stays. Longer tenure raises CLV (more time to earn revenue from them), which widens the healthy delta. So retention directly improves the CAC vs CLV balance, which loops straight back to CVM's focus on existing customers.
Key term. CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost). The total cost involved in acquiring a new customer. Growth is sustainable only while CAC < CLV.
Key term. k (customer tenure). How long a customer stays with the product or organization. Higher tenure raises CLV and widens the healthy CLV-minus-CAC delta.
Exam trap. The phrase to recall on demand is "Start-up Demise," and it is triggered by CAC > CLV (acquisition cost exceeds lifetime value). If you flip the inequality you flip the meaning. Healthy = CAC below CLV. Demise = CAC above CLV.

3. The Data Mix

Acting "in the moment": real-time context

The lecture frames the Data Mix around relevance in the moment: "You can incorporate real-time to make the experience relevant in the moment. Where are they? What device are they on? Is the item that they want in stock? Did it recently drop in price?" The headline takeaway is also stated directly: "the entire effort and context is the function of data available at hand." In plain terms, your ability to be contextual is capped by the data you actually have.

The four data sources: WHO / WHEN / WHAT / WHY

These four answer four different questions. Learn each source and the question it answers.

SourceQuestion it answersLecture detail to remember
WHO = DemographicsWho someone is"Still reigns king as the only available data targeting across media channels." Demographic data is the most universally usable for targeting.
WHEN = In-market timingWhen someone is in the market to buy"With the decline of the 3rd party cookie, it is becoming just as challenging to target when someone is in market digitally." The hard part is timing.
WHAT = Transaction dataWhat someone has bought"Your customer transaction data shows you what someone has bought from you in the past." This is your own first-party purchase history.

Exam trap. Match each letter to the right source: WHO = demographics, WHEN = in-market timing (hurt by the third-party cookie decline), WHAT = transaction data, WHY = surveys. A classic trick is to attach "surveys" to WHAT or "transactions" to WHY. Surveys = WHY (motivation); transactions = WHAT (past purchases).

4. Promotional vs Emotional Growth

Two types of persuasion

The lecture contrasts two growth types:

  • The Promotional Type. "Optimized for short term impact." Think discounts, coupons, limited-time offers. It spikes behavior now but the loyalty fades when the deal ends.
  • The Emotional Type. Described as "mass-marketed and expensive." It builds feeling and identity around the brand. It is costlier and broader, but it creates attachment.

The punchline the lecture wants you to remember: "Sustainable growth requires more emotional growth than rational or promotional persuasion. Engage customers emotionally and they will have stronger loyalty and value to your brand." So for durable growth, weight your mix toward emotional persuasion over promotional.

Exam trap. Promotional growth is the one "optimized for short term impact." Sustainable growth needs more emotional than promotional persuasion. Do not say discounts drive long-term loyalty; the lecture says the opposite. Emotional attachment, not promotions, produces lasting value.

5. Segmentation

WHAT it is, WHY we do it, HOW we do it

The lecture introduces segmentation with the question "Better understanding the customers, the first logical step?" and organizes it as WHAT / WHY / HOW.

  • WHY (the reasons). "Trying to market the entire population is expensive and covered via ATL use cases." Micro-segmentation techniques (decode every acronym)

The lecture lists these under micro-segmentation. Beginners must be able to expand each:

  • CLV (Customer Lifetime Value). Segment customers by how much total value they will bring. Group your highest-value customers together and treat them differently.
  • RFM (Recency, Frequency, Monetary). A classic scoring method: how recently a customer bought, how frequently they buy, and how much money they spend. Each customer gets a score on the three, and you segment from those scores.
  • IBRO. A behavior/value-based segmentation lens used alongside RFM and CLV. The lecture lists it as a micro-segmentation technique without expanding the letters, so treat it as a named technique in the same family (behavioral, actionable segmentation).

  • ML/DL driven (unsupervised). Using Machine Learning or Deep Learning in unsupervised mode, meaning the algorithm finds groupings in the data on its own without being told the labels in advance (clustering). The machine discovers the segments rather than you defining them.
Key term. ATL vs BTL. ATL (Above The Line) marketing is broad, mass-market advertising aimed at everyone (TV, radio, billboards); the lecture says marketing the entire population is "covered via ATL use cases." BTL (Below The Line) marketing is targeted, segment-specific activity (direct, personalized campaigns). You segment partly "to manage the available BTL budget," because targeted spend must be aimed carefully.
Exam trap. "Unsupervised" is the load-bearing word for ML/DL segmentation. Unsupervised = the model discovers groups without predefined labels (clustering). If a question describes the algorithm being given the right answers to learn from, that is supervised, which is not what segmentation uses here. Also keep ATL (broad/mass) vs BTL (targeted) straight: the whole point of segmentation is to spend BTL budget wisely instead of blasting everyone via ATL.

6. The Three Experiment Types

Why three different experiments exist

The lecture says: "When targeted via CVM, differentiation between the types of experiments becomes vital to accurate assessment." Each experiment isolates a different effect. If you only run one, you only learn one thing. The big idea on the next slide: "The total value creation that an organization can benefit from is the difference between not doing any targeted marketing campaign, and running all three types of experiments." In other words, Total value = (doing nothing) compared against (running all three experiments). The "do nothing" baseline is your reference point.

Experiment 1: Target vs Control (isolates the COMMUNICATION effect)

  • What it measures: "the communication effect," by comparing the response rate between two groups.
  • Target Group: customers who will receive the communication.
  • Control Group: customers who will not receive the communication.
  • The catch: "In all campaigns we aim to have control groups and under ideal circumstances, it should be based on stratified sampling so that its comparison with target stays apple to apple." If the two groups are not similar, any difference might be due to the groups, not the message.

Stratified sampling = splitting customers into matched strata (layers) first (by age, value, region, etc.) and then drawing both groups so they mirror each other. This makes the comparison "apple to apple," so a difference in response can be attributed to the communication itself.

Experiment 2: A/B Test (finds the BEST communication)

  • What it measures: "the best communication," by comparing the response rate between Target Group A (gets communication A) and Target Group B (gets communication B).
  • The method: "First, all communications are tested against each other (all challengers) until the identification of the best one (champion). Later, we test our champion against new ones (champion vs challengers)."
  • Champion vs challenger logic: the current best message is the champion; new candidate messages are challengers. You keep trying to dethrone the champion. If a challenger wins, it becomes the new champion.

Experiment 3: Model Performance (isolates the MODELLING effect)

  • What it measures: "the modelling effect," by comparing the response rate between a Random Group and a High Probability Group.
  • Random Group: "will act as the control group to measure the modelling effect, and will be based on a simple random selection of customers."
  • High Probability Group: "a selection of customers with high probability according to a propensity model, after the random group is chosen."
  • The point: if the model-picked (high-propensity) group responds much better than a random group, the model is adding value. That extra lift is the modelling effect.

Propensity model = a model (usually Machine Learning) that scores each customer by their likelihood (propensity) to take an action, for example to buy or to respond. High-propensity customers are the ones the model predicts are most likely to convert.

7. Hypothesis Testing EXAM-FLAGGED

Why we test hypotheses at all

The lecture asks "How can we trust the results?" and answers: "We are essentially running experiments where we are testing if we have been able to change the behavior (for example, cross-sell a product, customer downloads our mobile app) for a given group of customers with certain conditions (the communication)." Statistically, we are testing "whether or not a statement is true." This is how an experiment result turns into a trustworthy business decision instead of a lucky fluke.

Null vs Alternative hypothesis

  • Null Hypothesis (often written H0): "Sending this communication does not change the response rate." The null is the boring, "nothing happened" assumption. We assume it until the data forces us to drop it.
  • Alternative Hypothesis (often written H1): "Sending the communication does change the response rate." This is the claim that something real happened.
  • The decision: "Based on the results, the null hypothesis can be accepted (nothing has changed), or rejected in favor of the alternative hypothesis (something has changed) if there is enough statistical significance."

The two flavors of the alternative: directional vs non-directional

This is the part the lecture flags most carefully. The alternative hypothesis "has two flavors":

  • Directional (one-tailed): "Did this communication increase conversion? (not just change it). Only uplift counts!" You are testing for movement in one specific direction (up). A drop does not count as success.
  • Non-directional (two-tailed): "Did this communication change conversion (up or down)? Uplift or drop both count!" You are testing for movement in either direction. Both an increase and a decrease are "a change."
Key term. Statistical significance. Evidence strong enough that the observed result is very unlikely to be random chance. The lecture stresses: "Only with statistical significance will we be able to make decisions based on the results in our business strategy." No significance, no confident decision.
Exam trap. Memorize the pairing exactly: Directional = one-tailed = only uplift counts. Non-directional = two-tailed = up OR down both count. The most common mistake is swapping "one-tailed" and "two-tailed," or saying a directional test counts a drop. It does not, a directional test only rewards movement in the chosen direction (uplift). Also remember: you reject the null (not the alternative) when you find significance.

9. The AI Paradigms Stack HIGHEST PRIORITY

The one sentence that frames everything

The lecture header: "AI to Business Value BUT as Fit-for-Purpose and NOT as One-Size-Fits-All." Then the thesis: "AI Is a Stack, Not a Single Tool. Not Better or Worse, Just Different Strengths. Applied AI, Generative AI, and Agentic AI are designed for different decisions, not to replace one another." And later, the line to quote in any answer on this topic: "GenAI Doesn't Replace ML or Applied AI, It Expands the AI Toolbox."

  • AI (Artificial Intelligence) = computer systems that perform tasks normally needing human intelligence.
  • The mental model: AI is a stack of five layers. Each layer is best for a different kind of decision. Newer layers do not delete older ones; they add to the toolbox.
  • Three "fit" labels group the layers by how much independence they have: AI Assistive (helps you decide), AI Adaptive (responds to language and intent), and AI Autonomous (acts on its own).

Layer 1: Expert Systems, "The Enforcer"

  • What it is: "If X happens, Do Y." It applies known, hand-written rules with certainty.
  • Why it works: "Zero ambiguity." The rule is explicit, so the output is exact and predictable.
  • Examples: Compliance, Controls, Policy Enforcement.
  • Risk: "Brittle when change is frequent." If the world keeps changing, the fixed rules break and someone must keep rewriting them.

Layer 2: Machine Learning, "The Predictor" (AI Assistive)

  • What it is: "If X happened, Y has a probability to happen." It predicts what is likely.
  • Why it works: "Learns correlations and approximates causation." It finds patterns in past data and uses them to estimate future odds.
  • Examples: Credit scoring, propensity modeling, churn, NBA (Next Best Action, the recommended next move for a customer).
  • Risk: "Monitoring needed, degrades as data/environment changes." A model trained on yesterday's world slowly goes stale.

Layer 3: Deep Learning, "The Pattern Finder"

  • What it is: "When X is not clearly defined, it discovers patterns." It finds structure we did not explicitly program.
  • Why it works: "Learns representations automatically at scale." Using neural networks, it builds its own internal features from raw data.
  • Examples: Fraud and gaming detection, image processing.
  • Risk: "Opaque decisions, explainability challenges, hard to govern." It is a "black box," you may not be able to explain why it decided something.

Deep Learning (DL) is a sub-field of Machine Learning that uses many-layered neural networks. The lecture's "Right Fit" slide groups "DL, NN, Robotics" together (NN = Neural Networks).

Layer 4: Generative AI, "The Creator" (AI Adaptive)

  • What it is: "Given intent, generate X that never existed before." It produces new content.
  • Why it works: "Understands language, context, and semantics." It grasps meaning, not just patterns of numbers.
  • Examples: Conversation, summarization, reasoning, creativity.
  • Risk: "Hallucinations, inconsistency, needs oversight." It can confidently make things up, so a human must check it.

Generative AI (GenAI) = AI that creates new content (text, images, code) from learned knowledge. Hallucination = when a generative model produces a confident but false or made-up answer.

Layer 5: Agentic AI, "The Orchestrator" (AI Autonomous)

  • What it is: It "plans, decides, and takes action autonomously." It does not just answer, it acts.
  • Why it works: A "combo of reasoning, tools, memory, feedbacks." It can think, call tools, remember context, and learn from results across multiple steps.
  • Examples: End-to-end workflows, multi-step operations, auto-response.
  • Risk: "Mistakes faster automated, hard to control without guardrails." Because it acts on its own, a wrong decision is executed at speed, so guardrails are essential.

Agentic AI = AI that autonomously plans and executes multi-step tasks using reasoning plus tools plus memory plus feedback. A guardrail is a built-in limit or safety check that constrains what the system is allowed to do.

Grouping: what is "Applied AI"?

The first three layers are predictive/assistive and are grouped as Applied AI: Expert Systems + Machine Learning + Deep Learning. The two newer layers, Generative AI and Agentic AI, are the adaptive and autonomous frontiers. This is exactly why the lecture insists GenAI "expands the toolbox" rather than replacing ML or Applied AI: they sit at different layers of the same stack and serve different decisions.

10. GIGO: Garbage In, Garbage Out

The brutally true law of Data and AI

The lecture's closing law: "GIGO (Garbage IN to Garbage OUT). AI doesn't forgive dysfunction. It amplifies it. It scales what already exists, good or bad." Read the consequences exactly as stated:

  • "If your data is messy, AI scales the mess."
  • "If your processes are unclear, AI accelerates confusion."
  • "If your metrics are wrong, AI optimizes in the wrong direction."
  • "If the foundations are broken, AI makes things worse, faster."

And the memorable contrast: "AI doesn't reward intention. It rewards integrity. Not the integrity of your brand values. The integrity of your data." A fancy "AI Centre of Excellence" or a great slide deck will not save you, "It just does what it's told."

Key term. GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out). If you feed a system bad inputs (messy data, wrong metrics, broken processes), you get bad outputs, and AI makes it worse by scaling and accelerating the problem rather than fixing it.

Guardrails and human oversight are not optional

The final two slides drive the safety message home: "The guardrails are not optional. Autonomy without control == Risk." And: "While AI is a facilitator and encouraged to be utilized and benefited from, the human oversight does and will continue to hold a critical role." The closing promise: "when applied responsibly, with defined guardrails and human oversight, AI can drive measurable and sustainable transformation." This connects directly to Agentic AI's risk (automated mistakes) and to CVM's "embrace AI" imperative: embrace it, but with data integrity, guardrails, and a human in the loop.

Exam trap. The equation to quote is "Autonomy without control == Risk." GIGO is not about model quality, the lecture is explicit that "no model, no matter how advanced, will fix things" if the data is shaky. The fix is integrity of data plus guardrails plus human oversight, not a bigger model.

Long-answer questions

Q1. Compare Applied AI vs Generative AI vs Agentic AI. Define each, give the lecture's nickname and an example for each layer inside them, and explain the lecture's core claim about how they relate.

A. The lecture frames AI as a stack, not a single tool: "not better or worse, just different strengths," and the paradigms are "designed for different decisions, not to replace one another."

Applied AI is the assistive/predictive group made of three layers. (1) Expert Systems, "The Enforcer": applies known rules with certainty ("if X then Y"), zero ambiguity, used for compliance, controls, and policy enforcement; its risk is being brittle when change is frequent. (2) Machine Learning, "The Predictor" (labeled AI Assistive): "if X happened, Y has a probability," it learns correlations and approximates causation, used for credit scoring, propensity, churn, and NBA (Next Best Action); its risk is needing monitoring and degrading as data changes. (3) Deep Learning, "The Pattern Finder": discovers patterns not explicitly defined and learns representations at scale, used for fraud and gaming detection and image processing; its risk is opacity and weak explainability.

Generative AI, "The Creator" (labeled AI Adaptive): given intent, it generates new content that never existed before, because it understands language, context, and semantics; examples are conversation, summarization, reasoning, and creativity; its risk is hallucinations and inconsistency, so it needs oversight.

Agentic AI, "The Orchestrator" (labeled AI Autonomous): it plans, decides, and acts autonomously by combining reasoning, tools, memory, and feedback; examples are end-to-end workflows, multi-step operations, and auto-response; its risk is that mistakes are automated faster and it is hard to control without guardrails.

The core claim to state explicitly: GenAI does not replace ML or Applied AI; it expands the toolbox. The right approach is fit-for-purpose: pick the layer that matches the decision (a fixed rule needs an Expert System, a likelihood needs ML, hidden patterns need DL, content/language needs GenAI, autonomous multi-step work needs Agentic AI with guardrails).

Q2. Explain the three experiment types in CVM (Target vs Control, A/B test, Model Performance), the distinct effect each one isolates, and why the lecture says total value comes from running all three.

A. The lecture says differentiating experiment types is "vital to accurate assessment" because each isolates a different effect.

Target vs Control measures the communication effect. A Target Group receives the communication and a Control Group does not; comparing their response rates shows whether the message itself changed behavior. Ideally the control is built with stratified sampling so the comparison with target is "apple to apple," meaning the two groups are matched and any difference is due to the message, not to group differences.

A/B Test finds the best communication. Target Group A gets message A and Target Group B gets message B. First all messages are tested as challengers until the best (the champion) is found; then the champion is tested against new challengers. This answers "which message works best?" rather than "does a message work at all?"

Model Performance measures the modelling effect. A Random Group (a simple random selection acting as the control) is compared with a High Probability Group selected by a propensity model. If the model-selected group responds more strongly, the targeting model itself is adding value.

Finally, the lecture states "the total value creation an organization can benefit from is the difference between not doing any targeted marketing campaign, and running all three types of experiments." The "do nothing" baseline is the reference point; running all three (proving the message works, choosing the best message, and proving the model beats random) captures the full value, because each experiment unlocks a separate slice of lift.

Q3. Explain directional (one-tailed) vs non-directional (two-tailed) alternative hypotheses, the role of the null hypothesis, and why statistical significance matters for business decisions.

A. When we run a CVM experiment, we are testing whether a communication changed behavior (for example, getting customers to cross-sell or download the app). In statistical terms we test whether a statement is true.

The Null Hypothesis says "sending this communication does not change the response rate," that is, nothing happened. We assume it by default. The Alternative Hypothesis says "sending the communication does change the response rate," that is, something happened.

The alternative has two flavors. Directional (one-tailed) asks "did this communication increase conversion?" Only uplift counts; a decrease is not treated as success. Non-directional (two-tailed) asks "did this communication change conversion (up or down)?" Both an increase and a decrease count as a change.

Based on the results, the null is either accepted (nothing changed) or rejected in favor of the alternative (something changed) if there is enough statistical significance, meaning the result is unlikely to be random chance. The lecture stresses that "only with statistical significance will we be able to make decisions based on the results in our business strategy." Without significance, an apparent uplift could be noise, so it cannot safely drive a business decision.

Q4. Define CVM and explain how it connects to the CAC vs CLV balance and the 5 market imperatives. Why does the lecture say "when we talk about Growth, we talk about CVM"?

A. CVM (Customer Value Management) is a strategic, data-driven operating approach that maximizes profitability by increasing the CLV (Customer Lifetime Value) of existing customers rather than just acquiring new ones. It is about understanding not only who a customer is, but where they are, what they are doing, and what they are likely to do next, and then delivering the right service and communication at the precise moment it offers the most value.

It connects to the CAC vs CLV balance because CVM's focus on existing customers directly lifts CLV and lengthens tenure (k = customer tenure). A business should keep investing only while CAC < CLV; if CAC exceeds CLV the result is "Start-up Demise." By deepening existing relationships, CVM widens the healthy delta (CLV minus CAC) and keeps the model balanced.

It connects to the 5 market imperatives because the lecture says organizations must use modern CVM frameworks to preserve value around them: embrace the mobile mind shift, turn big data into business insights, transform the customer experience (CX), become a digital disruptor, and embrace and practice AI. These are the strategic moves that let a company actually capture lifetime value at scale.

The lecture equates growth with CVM because durable growth is not won by endless acquisition; it is won by maximizing the value of the customers you already have, in context, at the right moment, supported by data and AI. That is precisely what CVM operationalizes.

Q5. Explain the GIGO law and the lecture's stance on guardrails and human oversight. Why does the lecture say "no model, no matter how advanced, will fix things"?

A. GIGO (Garbage In, Garbage Out) is the lecture's "brutally true law of Data and AI": "AI doesn't forgive dysfunction, it amplifies it. It scales what already exists, good or bad." Concretely: messy data gets scaled into a bigger mess, unclear processes get accelerated into more confusion, and wrong metrics cause AI to optimize in the wrong direction. If the foundations are broken, AI makes things "worse, faster."

The lecture stresses that AI "rewards integrity, not intention," specifically the integrity of your data, not your brand values, mission statement, or "AI Centre of Excellence." Because AI "just does what it's told," a more powerful model cannot rescue bad inputs; it will simply execute the dysfunction at greater scale. That is why "no model, no matter how advanced, will fix things" when the data and processes are shaky.

The conclusion is a safety mandate: "guardrails are not optional. Autonomy without control == Risk." This ties to Agentic AI, whose risk is automating mistakes faster. The lecture closes that AI is a facilitator and human oversight will continue to hold a critical role: "when applied responsibly, with defined guardrails and human oversight, AI can drive measurable and sustainable transformation." So the fix is clean data, clear processes, correct metrics, guardrails, and a human in the loop, not a bigger model.

Scenario 1 (which AI paradigm fits and why?). A digital bank must enforce a strict regulatory rule: any single transaction above PKR 1,000,000 from a non-corporate account must be blocked and flagged for review, with zero exceptions and full auditability. Which AI paradigm fits best, and why? What is the main risk to watch?

Model answer. This fits Agentic AI ("The Orchestrator," AI Autonomous). The lecture describes Agentic AI as a system that "plans, decides, and takes action autonomously" using a "combo of reasoning, tools, memory, and feedback," with examples being "end-to-end workflows, multi-step ops, and auto-response," which is exactly this dispute pipeline (multi-step, tool-using, acting without a human at each step). Scenario 4 (experiments). A marketing manager ran a single campaign: she sent a new push notification to a list of high-value customers picked by her data-science model, saw a 12% response rate, and declared the campaign and the model a success. Using the lecture's three experiment types, explain what is wrong with her conclusion and how she should have designed the test.
Model answer. Her conclusion conflates effects that the lecture says must be isolated separately. A bare 12% response with no comparison group proves nothing, because there is no baseline ("do nothing") to compare against. (1) She needs a Target vs Control test to measure the communication effect: a control group that receives no push, ideally chosen by stratified sampling so the comparison is apple to apple. Only then can she attribute the lift to the message. (2) To claim the message itself is good, she needs an A/B test comparing this notification (and other challengers) to find the champion, rather than assuming her one message is best. (3) To claim the model is good, she needs a Model Performance test: compare her high-probability (model-picked) group against a random group; if the model group beats random, the model is adding value (the modelling effect). The lecture notes total value comes from running all three versus doing nothing, and that any "success" claim must rest on statistical significance, not a single uncompared number.
Scenario 5 (hypothesis testing). A product team tests whether a redesigned onboarding screen changes signup conversion. Leadership only cares about whether conversion went up, because they will roll out the new screen only if it improves results. Frame this as a hypothesis test: state the null and alternative, say whether it is directional or non-directional and why, and explain the role of statistical significance.
Model answer. Null hypothesis (H0): the redesigned onboarding screen does not change signup conversion. Alternative hypothesis (H1): the redesigned screen changes signup conversion. Because leadership only cares about an increase (they will roll out only if conversion improves), this is a directional (one-tailed) test: "did this change increase conversion?", where only uplift counts and a drop would not be treated as success. (If they cared about any movement up or down, it would be non-directional / two-tailed.) The team then checks statistical significance: only if the observed uplift is unlikely to be random chance can they reject the null in favor of the alternative and confidently decide to roll out. Without significance, an apparent improvement could be noise, and the lecture is explicit that only with statistical significance can results drive a real business decision.

How to read "relevance." Relevance means the offering solves a real need for a real customer better than the alternatives. Products vs Services

The two kinds of offering

The lecture splits everything a business can sell into two buckets:

  • Products are (i) tangible offerings that are (ii) put in the market for acquisition. "Tangible" means you can point at it or own a copy of it (a phone, a software license, a song file). "Acquisition" means the customer takes possession of it.
  • Services are (i) intangible offerings that are (ii) actions offered to customers for the purpose of a transaction. "Intangible" means there is nothing physical to hold; you experience it (a haircut, financial advice, a money transfer). The value is in the action being performed for you.

The exam point is not the dictionary definition. It is the closing logic: tangible or intangible, both survive or fail on usefulness. So the product-vs-service label is just a starting classification; relevance is what decides success.

THE KEY PAIR: Basic/MVP vs Augmented product (product layers)

Exam trap (highest priority). This exact pair was on the last sessional and is the single most likely thing to reappear. Memorise the layered picture, the two definitions, and one fintech example. The product as layers (an onion)

A product is not one flat thing. It has layers, drawn as rings around a centre. Working from the inside out (this is exactly the diagram on the "Types of Digital Products (2/2)" slide):

  • Core benefit (centre). The essential motivational benefit the customer is actually buying. It is the real reason they hand over money. The slide calls this the Core Product: "the essential motivational benefit the customer is purchasing, what the customer wants to get most."
  • Basic / MVP (middle ring). The minimum offering needed to deliver the core benefit that the customer expects from the product. The slide's diagram labels this ring the Actual Product: the physical and intangible properties the product takes on (materials, styling, branding, features) that the customer "actually gets to satisfy the desire for the core benefit." In plain words: the smallest real, usable product that still delivers the core benefit.
  • Augmented (outer ring). Features that go beyond the customer's minimum expectations. The slide says these are "extra attributes and features that are not part of the actual product but may be offered to make the offering more attractive, to speed purchase decisions, and to match or differentiate the actual product from the competition."

So the mental model is: Core benefit (why they buy) → Basic/MVP (the minimum real product that delivers it) → Augmented (the extras that go beyond expectations).

Key term: MVP. In this layered diagram, MVP means the Basic layer (the minimum offering that delivers the core benefit). In the broader product world, MVP also stands for Minimum Viable Product: the smallest version of a product you can put in front of real users to start learning and delivering value, with the least effort. Both readings agree: MVP is about the minimum that is still genuinely useful. If the exam uses "MVP" expand it as Minimum Viable Product and tie it to "minimum offering that delivers the core benefit."

The 8 precursor questions for designing a digital product

What "precursor" means and why these come first

A digital product exists for the purpose of a digital transaction (a transaction completed through digital channels). Before you jump into the formal design steps, the lecture says to interrogate the idea with eight questions. "Precursor" simply means "asked beforehand": these set the context so you do not design in a vacuum.

  1. Market need. Is there a genuine, sizeable need this product addresses? (Relevance again.)
  2. Strategy. How does this product fit the organisation's strategy and goals? What is the plan to win?
  3. Customer / Market segmentation. Which groups (segments) of customers are we serving? Segmentation = splitting the market into groups with shared needs/behaviour so you can target precisely.
  4. How does the digital world affect the product in consideration? What changes because it is digital (reach, speed, copying, data, distribution)?
  5. Digital customer journey. The end-to-end path a customer takes through digital touchpoints, from first awareness to purchase to ongoing use.
  6. Customer experience (CX). The overall quality of how it feels to discover, buy, and use the product across every interaction.
  7. How does the product enable electronic consumer relationships to create a network? Does it connect customers to each other or to the firm in ways that build a network (and network value)?
  8. Scale and the $ side. Can it grow to many users, and do the economics work (cost to serve, revenue, margin, breakeven)? This is where money enters the conversation.
Memory hook. Read the eight as a funnel from outside-in then back to money: Need → Strategy → Segments → Digital effect → Journey → Experience → Network → Scale and dollars. The first three frame the opportunity, the middle three are about the digital experience, the last two are about networks and economics.

The two "I"s of digital products: Interactivity and Individualization

The core idea

The headline on this slide is the most quotable line of the week: "The magic of digital products is not that they exist on screens. It is that they can react." Two properties make that reaction possible, both starting with "I".

  • Interactivity = the product responds to the user, and the firm responds back. Specifically it:
    • Allows responsive service interactions that provide augmented value to the product (the back-and-forth itself adds value).
    • Lets organisations quickly gather valuable customer data (e.g. preferences) from how people use it.
    • Brings customers directly into the product development (their behaviour and feedback shape the next version).
  • Individualization = the product can treat each person differently. Specifically it:
    • Makes it easy to convey prices to individuals (show the right price to the right person).
    • Allows more targeted price promotions.
    • Lets different presences cater to different segments (different versions/pages for different groups).
    • Supports dynamic pricing presences that keep individuals involved (prices that change by demand, time, or person).
Plain-English contrast. Interactivity is about two-way conversation (the product reacts to YOU, and the firm learns from everyone). Individualization is about one-to-one tailoring (the firm reacts to EACH person with custom prices, offers, and experiences). Interactivity feeds the data; individualization spends that data on personalised treatment.
Fintech illustration. Interactivity: a banking app logs that you always check your balance before payday and that you tap "transfer" most on Fridays, then surfaces a quick-transfer shortcut. Individualization: the bank offers YOU a specific loan rate and a targeted "save AED 50/month" nudge based on your transaction history, while a different customer sees a different rate and a different nudge. Dynamic pricing example: surge-free instant-transfer fee that drops during low-traffic hours to keep you engaged.

The Digital Product Portfolio

Firms build portfolios of bets, not single products

The lecture frames it memorably: "Organizations do not just build products, they build portfolios of bets. Some bets improve what already exists. Others stretch the line. A few redefine the game." A portfolio here means the full set of products a firm runs at once, deliberately spread across different risk levels. The diagram plots this on an axis of Degree of Innovation, from None to Incremental to Breakthrough/Discontinuous:

  • Improve what exists (low innovation, "None"). These are Existing Products: keep refining the current offering. Lowest risk, lowest novelty.
  • Stretch the line (Incremental innovation). These are Line Extensions: new variants/features built on top of what you have (e.g. a premium tier of an existing account). Medium risk.
  • Redefine the game (Breakthrough / Discontinuous innovation). These are New Products that are new to the organisation, new to the market, and use new technology. Highest risk, highest potential payoff.

The strategic point: a healthy portfolio mixes all three. Too many safe bets and you get out-innovated; too many moonshots and you run out of money before any pays off.

Entry order into the digital market and the S-curve

When you enter matters

The lecture states: "First to market and fast followers often have more sustainability. Digital product lifecycle has a similar S curve." The diagram shows a "Hockey Stick Adoption Curve" (market adoption against time) with entrants placed along it:

  • First to market. The earliest entrant, present at the very bottom of the curve before adoption takes off. Can build a strong, lasting (sustainable) position by defining the category and locking in early users.
  • Fast follower. Enters quickly after the pioneer, just as the curve steepens. Often also achieves strong sustainability: it learns from the pioneer's mistakes while the market is still young.
  • "Me Too!" Enters during the steep growth, copying what already works. Less differentiated.
  • "Slackers." Enter late, near the top/flattening of the curve, when most growth is already captured. Hardest position to win from.
Key term: S-curve (product lifecycle). An S-curve plots cumulative adoption (or sales) over time. It is slow at first (few early adopters), then steep (rapid mainstream adoption), then flattens (the market saturates). It is "S" shaped because of those three phases. The takeaway: entering early (first-to-market or fast follower) puts you on the steep part with room to grow, which is why those positions tend to be more sustainable.
Exam trap. Do not over-claim that first-to-market always wins. The lecture says first movers AND fast followers "often have more sustainability." Fast followers are explicitly included. If asked "is it always best to be first," the nuanced answer is: being early (pioneer or fast follower) usually beats being late ("Me Too" or "Slackers"), but fast followers can outdo pioneers by learning from their mistakes.

THE 8-STEP PRODUCT DESIGN PROCESS (ideation to commercialization)

The process at a glance

The Product Development Process is an 8-step process that "includes activities needed to bring a new concept to a state of market readiness." Memorise the order. A clean memory sentence (first letters): Generate, Screen, Concept-test, Marketing/Business analysis, Prototype, Iterate (test), Commercialize, Breakeven. ("Good Students Can Make Products, Iterate, Commercialize, Breakeven.")

  1. Idea Generation. Create lots of candidate ideas, then narrow them. This is where you allow divergent and convergent thinking: divergent = open up and generate many possibilities; convergent = narrow down to the best. Inputs: customer and competitor research, brainstorm sessions, surveys, customer inputs, and R&D with relevant business stakeholders.
  2. Idea Screening. Filter the ideas by asking three hard questions: (a) Is there a real, clearly defined customer need for this product or service? (b) Can we fully develop this product? (c) Is the profit potential large enough to justify its development? Anything that fails these is dropped now (cheaply).
  3. Concept Development and Testing. A research method that asks customers about the concept of the product beforehand, before any tangible build. The principal target is to quantify actual customer enthusiasm. You get a solid feel only if the buyers are genuinely interested in the concept before any real development happens. (Cheap proof of demand before spending money building.)
  4. Marketing Strategy and Business Analysis. Put numbers and a plan around it: a formal list of features and benefits and the key USP (Unique Selling Proposition: the one reason a customer picks you over rivals); a summary of all resources needed for development; a marketing plan with potential ROI (Return on Investment: profit relative to cost) and projections across key KPIs (Key Performance Indicators: the metrics you will track); and a summary of competitive products, pricing, and packaging.
  5. Product Prototype Development. Build a tangible version. The product name and specific marketing messaging are solidified. You show it to potential prospects/buyers and ask: do you like it, would you buy it, and what would make it better? A tangible option is achieved at this step (the first time there is something real to hold or click).
  6. Iterative Testing. Refine in loops. Bugs are worked out; you take test results and user feedback from previous changes and test them against predefined baseline metrics (the targets you set in advance); designs are refined. "Iterative" means repeating the build-test-learn loop until the metrics are met.
  7. Commercialization. The product is finalized and launched full scale (full production and go-to-market). This is the real launch to the whole target market.
  8. Breakeven analysis and projections. Do the money math: prove when revenue will cover costs and lay out the financial projections that justify the whole effort (covered in detail in the next section).

Why Breakeven Analysis is critical in all projections

What breakeven means (from scratch)

The lecture says "Breakeven Analysis is super critical in all projections." The breakeven point (BEP) is the level of sales at which total revenue exactly equals total cost, so profit is zero. Below it you lose money; above it you make money. It answers the simplest survival question: "how much do we need to sell just to stop losing money?"

To get there you split costs into two kinds:

  • Fixed costs = costs that do not change with how much you sell (e.g. servers, salaries, rent). On the chart these "start at 0 sales" and stay flat (a horizontal line).
  • Variable costs = costs that rise with each extra unit sold (e.g. payment-processing fees per transaction). Total cost = fixed + variable, so the total-cost line slopes upward and starts above zero (because fixed costs exist even at zero sales).

Revenue is a line from the origin (zero sales = zero revenue) that slopes up with each unit sold. Where the revenue line crosses the total-cost line is the breakeven point. On the lecture's chart that crossing is the Break Even Point at 150 units: below 150 is the "loss making area," above 150 is the "profit making area."

The simple formula: Breakeven units = Fixed Costs / (Price per unit − Variable cost per unit). The bottom part (price minus variable cost) is the contribution margin: how much each sale contributes toward covering fixed costs.

Why it is "critical in all projections." Every revenue projection is just a hopeful story until you know the breakeven. It tells you (1) the minimum scale you must reach to survive, (2) how risky the plan is (a high breakeven means you need lots of sales before any profit), and (3) when the product turns from cash-draining to cash-generating. This is why it is the last of the 8 steps: it converts the whole design effort into a go/no-go money decision.

Using an average monthly churn rate of 4% (churn = the share of customers who leave each period), an average customer lifetime of 12.5 months, and an ARPU of AED 350 (ARPU = Average Revenue Per User, the average revenue one customer brings per period), the deck computes expected revenue from "rescued" (retained) customers as customers retained x 12.5 x 350. Long-answer questions (with full model answers)

Q1 (MANDATORY PAIR). Differentiate the Basic/MVP product from the Augmented product, including the product-layers model, and illustrate with an example.

A. A product can be pictured as concentric layers around a centre. The centre is the core benefit: the essential motivation the customer is buying (the real reason they pay). Wrapped around it is the Basic/MVP layer (the slide's "Actual Product"): the minimum offering, including the necessary physical and intangible properties, features, styling, and branding, needed to actually deliver that core benefit. The outer ring is the Augmented layer: extra attributes and features that are not part of the basic product but are added to make the offering more attractive, speed up purchase decisions, and differentiate it from competitors. In short, Basic/MVP is the FLOOR (the least that still delivers the core benefit) while Augmented is the CEILING (everything BEYOND the customer's minimum expectations).

The two differ in purpose and risk. The Basic/MVP exists to meet expectations and deliver the core benefit (and, read as Minimum Viable Product, to learn fast with least effort); its risk is being too thin to deliver the benefit (immediate churn) or, if over-built, no longer being minimal (slow, costly launch). The Augmented layer exists to delight and differentiate at low cost; its risk is feature bloat (complexity and cost) if over-done, or no differentiation (easy to copy, price competition) if under-done.

Example (digital wallet). Core benefit: move and hold money safely and instantly. Basic/MVP: secure login, balance view, send/receive money, transaction history (without these the core benefit is not delivered). Augmented: round-up savings, real-time fraud alerts, spend analytics, cashback, instant card freeze. A wallet with only augmented extras but a broken "send" feature delivers no core benefit, which is exactly why the basic layer must come first and the augmented layer second. (Note: MVP also stands for Minimum Viable Product, the smallest releasable version used to learn from real users; both readings agree it is the minimum that is still genuinely useful.)

Q2 (MANDATORY). Put the 8 steps of product design in order and explain each.

A. The product development process brings a new concept to market readiness in eight ordered steps.
1) Idea Generation: produce many candidate ideas using divergent then convergent thinking, fed by customer and competitor research, brainstorms, surveys, customer inputs, and R&D with business stakeholders.
2) Idea Screening: filter ideas against three questions: is there a real, clearly defined customer need; can we fully develop it; is the profit potential large enough to justify development. Weak ideas are killed cheaply here.
3) Concept Development and Testing: ask customers about the concept beforehand to quantify their actual enthusiasm, getting a solid feel for demand before any tangible build.
4) Marketing Strategy and Business Analysis: write the formal list of features, benefits, and key USP; summarise resources needed; build a marketing plan with ROI and KPI projections; and summarise competitor products, pricing, and packaging.
5) Product Prototype Development: solidify the product name and marketing messaging and build a tangible version; show it to prospects and ask if they like it, would buy it, and what would improve it. A tangible option is achieved here.
6) Iterative Testing: work out bugs, refine designs, and test feedback from previous changes against predefined baseline metrics, looping until targets are met.
7) Commercialization: finalize the product and launch it full scale to the whole target market.
8) Breakeven analysis and projections: do the money math, finding the breakeven point and laying out financial projections to justify the effort.

The logic of the order: prove demand cheaply (steps 1 to 3) and plan the business (step 4) BEFORE spending money to build a prototype (step 5), then refine (6), launch (7), and confirm the economics (8).

Q3. Explain Products vs Services as defined in the lecture, and explain why the lecture insists "the market is not sentimental, it rewards relevance."

A. Products are tangible offerings put in the market for acquisition: the customer takes possession of something (a card, a downloadable app). Services are intangible offerings, that is, actions offered to customers for the purpose of a transaction (a transfer, financial advice); there is nothing physical to own, only an action experienced. The distinction blurs in the digital world, where a "digital service" like banking is an action delivered through a product-like app. The lecture's point is that the label is not what determines success: "both survive or fail on usefulness." "The market is not sentimental" means buyers do not reward effort, cleverness, or beauty for their own sake; they reward "relevance," meaning the offering must solve a genuine need better than the alternatives. A gorgeous app nobody needs fails; a plain one that solves a painful need succeeds. This is why every precursor question (starting with "market need") and the first screening question (real, clearly defined customer need) hammer on relevance.

Q4. Describe the two "I"s of digital products and give a fintech example of each. Why do the two work together?

A. The two "I"s explain why digital products are powerful: they can react. Interactivity means the product responds to the user and the firm responds back: it allows responsive service interactions that add augmented value, lets the organisation quickly gather valuable customer data such as preferences, and brings customers directly into product development. Fintech example: a banking app notices you check your balance every payday and adds a quick-transfer shortcut, while usage data shapes the next release. Individualization means treating each person differently: it makes it easy to convey prices to individuals, allows more targeted price promotions, lets different presences cater to different segments, and supports dynamic pricing that keeps individuals engaged. Fintech example: the bank offers you a personalised loan rate and a tailored savings nudge based on your transaction history, different from what another customer sees. They work together because interactivity FEEDS the data (it learns who you are and what you do), and individualization SPENDS that data (it tailors prices, offers, and experiences to you). Without interactivity there is no data to personalise with; without individualization the gathered data is never acted upon.

Q5. What is breakeven analysis, why is it "super critical in all projections," and how would you compute the breakeven point? Tie it to the fintech projections math in the deck.

A. Breakeven analysis finds the breakeven point, the sales level at which total revenue exactly equals total cost, so profit is zero. Below it the business loses money; above it, it makes money. To compute it you split costs into fixed costs (unchanging with volume, such as servers and salaries, flat from zero sales) and variable costs (rising per unit, such as per-transaction processing fees). Revenue starts at zero and rises with each sale; the breakeven point is where the revenue line crosses the total-cost line (in the lecture chart, at 150 units, dividing the loss-making area below from the profit-making area above). The formula is Breakeven units = Fixed Costs / (Price per unit minus Variable cost per unit), where the denominator is the contribution margin (how much each sale contributes to covering fixed costs). It is "super critical in all projections" because any revenue forecast is only a hopeful story until you know the minimum scale needed to survive; breakeven reveals how risky the plan is and when the product turns cash-positive, which is why it is the final step of the 8-step process. In the deck this links to fintech projections: a credit-headroom upsell estimate projects extra revenue from each customer's payment potential (highest minus average paid, around 367) scaled across 100,000 customers and haircut by a 0.5% capture rate (about 2.2 million per year), and a churn-reduction estimate values retained customers at retained x average lifetime (12.5 months) x ARPU (AED 350), for example 200 x 12.5 x 350 = AED 875,000. These projections only mean something once compared against the cost base through breakeven analysis.

Q6. List and briefly explain the 8 precursor questions for designing a digital product, and explain what "scale and the $ side" adds.

A. Before the formal design steps, eight questions frame the opportunity: (1) Market need: is there a genuine, sizeable need; (2) Strategy: how it fits the organisation's plan to win; (3) Customer/Market segmentation: which groups of customers, split by shared needs, we serve; (4) How the digital world affects the product: what changes because it is digital (reach, speed, copying, data); (5) Digital customer journey: the end-to-end path through digital touchpoints from awareness to use; (6) Customer experience: the overall quality of every interaction; (7) How the product enables electronic consumer relationships to create a network: whether it connects customers to each other or the firm to build network value; (8) Scale and the $ side: whether it can grow to many users and whether the economics work. "Scale and the $ side" adds the commercial reality check: a digital product can in principle serve millions cheaply (especially digital services, where producer and consumer are separated in time and space), but you must confirm the cost-to-serve, revenue, margin, and breakeven actually support that growth. It connects the design conversation to money, which is then formalised in the business analysis and breakeven steps.

Pricing Strategies & the Pricing Game IN SCOPE

Pricing is how you decide what to charge for your product. It is one of the highest-leverage decisions a Product Manager (PM, the person who decides what a product does and how it goes to market) can make, because a change in price flows straight to profit without needing you to build anything new. This section has two halves. Part A walks through the nine pricing strategies from the reading, one by one. Part B explains the "Pricing Games" Excel model from scratch and works a full numeric solution, because a quantitative pricing question is very likely on the exam.

Key term. Marginal cost. The cost of producing one more unit of your product. For a physical good (a coffee, a car) the marginal cost is real: you pay for materials and labour each time. For a digital product (software, an app, a streaming title) the marginal cost is close to zero, because copying a file costs almost nothing. The reading hammers this point repeatedly: because copying is virtually free, digital products are easy to scale, distribute, and maintain, so their costs grow slower than their revenue. This single fact decides which pricing strategies make sense for software.

Key term. Perceived value. What the customer believes the product is worth to them, which can be far higher (or lower) than what it cost you to make. Several strategies below try to charge based on perceived value rather than cost. This is where the big profit lives for digital products.

Part A. The Nine Pricing Strategies

The reading opens with one framing note: some of these strategies can be combined (Apple, for example, uses Premium and Value-Based together; Tesla uses Value-Based and Penetration together). Read each strategy below as a tool in a toolbox, not as a mutually exclusive choice. For each strategy you get a one-line definition, when to use it, the real example from the reading, and how it relates to digital products.

1. Cost-Plus Pricing

Definition (one line). Set the selling price by taking your total cost and adding a fixed amount or percentage on top (your markup).

When to use it. When you have real, tangible per-unit costs you can measure. Common in physical products, retail, and manufacturing.

Reading example. Consulting firms like McKinsey, Deloitte, and Accenture charge based on the hourly rates of their consultants. This is sometimes called "time and material" (you bill for the time spent plus the materials used).

Digital relevance. Weak. Because the marginal cost of a digital product is near zero, "cost plus a markup" would price the product almost at zero, which throws away most of the value you could capture. The reading explicitly says low marginal cost makes cost-plus pricing less relevant for digital products.

2. Competitive Pricing

Definition (one line). Set your price mainly by looking at what competitors charge for a similar product.

When to use it. In a crowded market with near-identical products, as a quick reference point. For digital products the lower marginal cost gives you more room to flex your price up or down against rivals.

Reading example / warning. The reading does not name a brand here; instead it gives a warning. Competing on price in a digital space is often a terrible idea because it drives every competitor down to extremely low margins. This race to the bottom is called the red ocean (a sea turned red by everyone bloodying each other on price). The better move is to compete on uniqueness: solve a specific customer problem better than anyone, give a superior user experience, or offer frictionless onboarding (a sign-up so smooth there is no friction stopping the user).

Digital relevance. Dangerous if used naively. Because digital products are easy to copy and undercut, pure price competition collapses margins for everyone. Differentiate instead.

Exam trap. If a question asks "why is competitive (price-matching) pricing especially dangerous for a digital product?", the answer is the red ocean: near-zero marginal cost means rivals can keep cutting price toward zero, so everyone ends up on razor-thin margins. The reading's prescribed alternative is to compete on uniqueness (better problem-solving, UX, onboarding), not on price.

3. Penetration Pricing

Definition (one line). Enter a market with a deliberately low price to grab customers and market share fast, then raise the price later once you are established.

When to use it. When launching a new product into a market that already has competitors, and you need to win users and build loyalty quickly.

Reading examples. Netflix launched streaming at a low price to pull in subscribers, then gradually raised prices once it had a large base. Uber entered markets worldwide with heavy discounts to pull riders away from taxis, then reduced the promotions over time.

Digital relevance. Strong fit. Near-zero marginal cost means the company can afford to serve many low-paying or free users early without large per-user losses, which makes "go cheap to grow, monetize later" practical.

4. Dynamic Pricing (surge / demand pricing)

Definition (one line). Set flexible prices that move up and down with current demand, also called surge pricing or demand pricing.

When to use it. When you have limited "inventory" (a fixed number of seats, rooms, or slots) that you want to sell at the highest price the market will bear. Common in travel, entertainment, and conferences.

Reading examples. Booking.com (hotel rooms) and American Airlines (flight seats). Prices rise as the date nears or demand climbs.

Digital relevance. Weak. Digital products usually do not have a limited inventory (you can sell unlimited copies), so the scarcity that makes dynamic pricing work is missing. The reading says dynamic pricing is less relevant for digital products.

5. Price Discrimination (including Location-Based Pricing)

Definition (one line). Charge different customers different prices for the same product, often based on the maximum each is willing to pay.

When to use it. When different groups of customers value the product differently or can afford different amounts, and you can separate them (by region, segment, or status). Location-based pricing is one named type: price by the buyer's country.

Reading example. Justin Welsh offers location-based discounts on his "Content Operating System", for example a 50% discount for Poland. He used a tool called ParityDeals to set this up. The reading notes it could not be tricked by popular VPNs (Virtual Private Networks, tools that hide your real location); the author tested it with AtlasVPN.

Digital relevance. Strong fit, and it pairs naturally with Value-Based Pricing. Because the marginal cost is near zero, selling the same file at a lower price in a poorer region still earns pure profit, while charging more in a richer region captures extra value. You lose almost nothing by serving both.

6. Economy Pricing

Definition (one line). Keep the price as low as possible by stripping out marketing and frills, targeting a large, very price-sensitive audience.

When to use it. When you serve a big mass market that cares mostly about price, and you have an efficient operation (great supply chain, economies of scale) that lets you stay profitable at low prices.

Reading examples. Walmart ("Everyday Low Prices", powered by an efficient supply chain and economies of scale). IKEA (efficient production and logistics, flat-pack furniture, customer self-service). Southwest Airlines (one aircraft type, the Boeing 737; no assigned seating, free meals, or first class; sells mostly direct from its website to avoid travel-agent commissions).

Key term. Economies of scale. The cost-per-unit drops as you produce more, because fixed costs are spread over more units and you get bulk efficiencies.

Digital relevance. Usually not the best fit. The reading notes it is easier to achieve product differentiation with digital products, so competing as the cheapest option is rarely the smartest play.

7. Premium Pricing

Definition (one line). Deliberately price higher than competitors to signal and cultivate a perception of superior quality. The opposite of Economy Pricing.

When to use it. When your brand, quality, design, or service genuinely stands above rivals and customers will pay extra for that status and experience.

Reading examples. Luxury brands like Apple, Rolex, and Gucci.

Caveat from the reading. Premium pricing can boost profitability, but it must be justified by delivering exceptional value, quality, customer service, and brand prestige. Charging more without backing it up will fail.

Digital relevance. Works well when paired with Value-Based Pricing (see Apple in strategy 9), because a strong brand lets you charge a premium on a product that costs almost nothing to copy.

8. Loss Leader Strategy

Definition (one line). Sell one product at a loss (below profit) on purpose, to attract customers or to sell other profitable products and services.

When to use it. When the first product locks the customer into an ecosystem or a stream of follow-on purchases that more than make up the loss.

Reading examples. Amazon sold Kindle e-readers at a loss, expecting to recover it through digital sales (eBooks, movies, music) and Prime memberships. Gillette sells razors cheaply but prices the replacement blades high, which customers must keep buying. This is the classic "razors-and-blades" model: give away the handle, profit on the refills.

Digital relevance. Very common in software as the "freemium" or hardware-plus-content model: hook users cheaply or free, then monetize ongoing digital purchases or subscriptions where the marginal cost is near zero.

9. Value-Based Pricing (the "holy grail")

Definition (one line). Set the price on the perceived value of the product to the customer, that is, what they are willing to pay for the benefit, not what it cost you to make.

When to use it. When you deeply understand your customers and the value they perceive, and you want to capture the largest possible share of that value. Especially powerful for software.

Why the reading loves it. Four stated advantages: (a) Focuses on customer value, charging what customers will pay rather than basing price on cost; (b) Maximizes profitability, capturing a larger portion of the value created, which is huge for software where marginal cost is virtually zero; (c) Flexibility, prices can differ by segment based on perceived value (the reading cites Microsoft's education offering); (d) Customer-centricity, it forces a deep understanding of customer needs, which leads to better products and experience.

Reading examples. Apple (combines Premium and Value-Based; strong brand and perceived value around quality, design, and user experience). Tesla (priced on perceived value: buyers purchase a high-end, environmentally friendly lifestyle and a future vision; note the nuance that Tesla is perceived as premium yet also uses Penetration Pricing to expand the EV market). Diamonds (value rests almost entirely on perceived value and scarcity; diamonds are valuable mainly because we believe they are).

Why it is the "holy grail". It is highly desirable but hard to achieve. It needs in-depth market research to learn how customers perceive value and a specific strategy to capture that value.

Digital relevance. The best fit of all. Because software has near-zero marginal cost, pricing on value (not cost) lets you capture far more profit than cost-plus ever could.

Key term. Cost leader vs differentiator. Two broad ways to win. A cost leader competes by being the cheapest (Economy Pricing fits here). A differentiator competes by being uniquely better and charges accordingly (Premium and Value-Based fit here). The reading asks you to consciously pick which one is your primary strategy.

Which strategies combine, and the conclusion checklist

Strategies that combine well (from the reading).

  • Apple = Premium + Value-Based. A high price (premium) justified by perceived value in design, quality, and experience.
  • Tesla = Value-Based + Penetration. Priced on perceived value of a lifestyle and vision, while also lowering prices over time to grow the electric-vehicle market.
  • Price Discrimination / Location-Based + Value-Based. Charge each segment by the value it perceives (different prices, same product).
  • Loss Leader + ongoing monetization. Amazon Kindle at a loss, then profit on eBooks and Prime; Gillette razors cheap, blades expensive.
Key term. The conclusion checklist (memorize this). The reading ends with six things you must do to choose a pricing strategy:
  1. Understand your cost structure and how it might change as you grow.
  2. Understand what customers value (use market research, surveys, interviews).
  3. Understand what customers are trying to achieve (their "jobs", that is, the underlying task they hire your product to do).
  4. Select your primary strategy: cost leader vs differentiator.
  5. Be clear about your value proposition and what is unique about it.
  6. Analyze your competitors, including substitute products (different products that solve the same need).
And the closing rule: pricing is not a set-it-and-forget-it decision. Test different approaches, regularly analyze how customers respond, and be ready to adjust. Pricing is strategic and worth investing time to get right.

Part B. The "Pricing Games" Excel model (from scratch)

The exercise is a spreadsheet called Pricing Games.xlsx, sheet "A bit on the Pricing". It models a subscription business with four customer segments and asks you to tune prices to hit a revenue target. We build it up piece by piece so you can recreate or solve it by hand in the exam.

The setup: four customer segments

The business has four segments of customers, labelled A, B, C, D. Think of them as four tiers (for example, big enterprise clients down to small individual users). Each segment has a number of customers and a subscription amount. Here is the raw data:

SegmentNumber of Customers (col C)Prod. Subscription Amt (col D)
A10025,000
B8020,000
C7010,000
D505,000
Total30060,000

Number of Customers (column C) is how many paying customers are in that tier. They sum to 300 (cell C7 = SUM(C3:C6)).

Product Subscription Amount (column D) is the size of the subscription contract associated with that tier (a base amount the pricing rate is applied to). They sum to 60,000 (cell D7 = SUM(D3:D6)).

V% (column E): each segment's share of the subscription base

Formula: V% = D / D7 (each segment's subscription amount divided by the total subscription amount of 60,000). In Excel this is =D3/$D$7. The dollar signs ($) "lock" the reference to cell D7 so it does not move when you copy the formula down.

SegmentDV% = D / 60,000
A25,0000.4167 (41.67%)
B20,0000.3333 (33.33%)
C10,0000.1667 (16.67%)
D5,0000.0833 (8.33%)

Why V% matters. It tells you how much of the subscription "weight" each segment carries. A and B together are 75% of the base, so any price move on A or B moves the total far more than the same move on C or D. V% is your at-a-glance guide to where the leverage is.

Current Pricing rate (column F) and Current Revenue (column G)

Current Pricing (column F) is the rate currently charged for each segment (a fraction applied to the subscription amount, like a percentage fee). The four current rates are:

  • A: 0.1164
  • B: 0.09
  • C: 0.075
  • D: 0.05

Current Revenue (column G) is the money each segment brings in today. The formula is:

Current Revenue (G) = Number of Customers (C) x Subscription Amount (D) x Current Pricing rate (F)

A: 100 x 25,000 x 0.1164 = 291,000
B:  80 x 20,000 x 0.0900 = 144,000
C:  70 x 10,000 x 0.0750 =  52,500
D:  50 x  5,000 x 0.0500 =  12,500
-----------------------------------------
TOTAL current revenue (G7)         = 500,000

So the business currently earns 500,000 (cell G7 = SUM(G3:G6)). This is the number every target is measured against.

The levers you control: New Pricing (I), p.p. (J), the orange 5% drop (L), New Revenue (M), Upside (N)

The right-hand side of the sheet is the "what if" zone. You change one input and watch revenue move.

  • New Pricing (column I) is the new rate you choose for each segment. This is the lever you tune. It plays the same role as column F, but it is the future price.
  • p.p. (column J) means "price-point change" = New Pricing minus Current Pricing = I minus F. In Excel =I3-F3. It simply shows how much you moved the rate up (positive) or down (negative) for each segment. p.p. is read as "percentage points", the raw difference between two rates.
  • The orange rule (column K header). The sheet lets you mark a segment "orange". An orange segment has its Subscription Amount cut by 5%. The header reads "Apply Subscription Amt. drop by 5% for Orange segments". This models a discount or concession you give to win that segment's agreement to a price change.
  • New Subscription Amount (column L) = D x 0.95 if the segment is orange, else just D. In Excel an orange cell is =D3*0.95 and a normal cell is =D3.
  • New Revenue / "New Pricing Sensitivity" (column M) = Number of Customers x New Subscription Amount x New Pricing = C x L x I. In Excel =L3*I3*C3. This is the future revenue for that segment.
  • Rev Upside (column N) = New Revenue minus Current Revenue = M minus G. In Excel =M3-G3. Positive means you gained money, negative means you lost it.

At the bottom: L9 = L7 minus D7 ("sub. Amt. impact", the total change in subscription base), M9 = M7 / G7 minus 1 ("Rev change %", the percentage change in total revenue), and N9 = SUM(N3:N6) ("total rev. upside", the total money gained).

Key term. Why p.p. and V% matter together. V% tells you which segment is worth touching (high V% = high leverage). p.p. tells you how hard you pushed the price there. The biggest revenue gains come from a meaningful positive p.p. applied to a high-V% segment (A, then B). A large p.p. on segment D barely moves the total because D is only 8.33% of the base.

The current state of the sheet (before you solve it)

The spreadsheet ships with some values already typed into New Pricing (column I): A = 0.14, B = 0.09, C = 0.08, D = 0.05, and only A marked orange. Let us compute where that lands:

A (orange): L = 25,000 x 0.95 = 23,750 ;  M = 100 x 23,750 x 0.14 = 332,500
B (normal): L = 20,000           ;  M =  80 x 20,000 x 0.09 = 144,000
C (normal): L = 10,000           ;  M =  70 x 10,000 x 0.08 =  56,000
D (normal): L =  5,000           ;  M =  50 x  5,000 x 0.05 =  12,500
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
New total revenue (M7)                                       = 545,000

Rev change % (M9) = 545,000 / 500,000 - 1 = 0.09 = +9.0%

So out of the box the sheet is only at +9% (545,000). The task is to push it to +20%. It is not yet at target, which is the whole point of the exercise.

The task, stated clearly

Task (cell B12). "Play with the new pricing (column I) in a way that earns you a 20% increase in the revenue." A 20% increase on 500,000 means reaching 600,000 in total new revenue (M7).

Condition (cell B13). "Only two segments can have orange boxes in column J." In other words, at most two of the four segments may take the 5% subscription drop. You cannot make all four orange.

Restated in plain English: choose New Pricing rates (column I) for A, B, C, D, and pick at most two segments to be orange, so that the four New Revenues add up to 600,000 (a +20% gain over 500,000).

Exam trap. Making a segment "orange" reduces revenue, because it cuts the subscription amount by 5% (you now multiply by 0.95). So an orange segment only helps if the New Pricing increase on it is large enough to more than compensate for the 5% drop. Do not assume "orange" is a bonus. It is a cost you must out-price. This is exactly why the condition limits you to two orange segments: each one is a handicap you have to overcome with a bigger price rise.
Scenario (the main worked solution). Tune column I to reach a +20% total (600,000), using at most two orange segments. Show all arithmetic.
Worked answer. Make A and B the two orange segments (they carry the most weight, V% of 41.67% and 33.33%, so they have the most leverage). Leave C and D exactly as they are today. Set New Pricing A = 0.16 and New Pricing B = 0.102. 
STEP 1 - New Subscription Amounts (column L), 5% drop on the orange ones:
   A (orange): L = 25,000 x 0.95 = 23,750
   B (orange): L = 20,000 x 0.95 = 19,000
   C (normal): L = 10,000
   D (normal): L =  5,000

STEP 2 - New Revenue per segment (M = C x L x I):
   A: 100 x 23,750 x 0.160 = 380,000
   B:  80 x 19,000 x 0.102 = 155,040
   C:  70 x 10,000 x 0.075 =  52,500   (unchanged)
   D:  50 x  5,000 x 0.050 =  12,500   (unchanged)

STEP 3 - Add them up:
   380,000 + 155,040 + 52,500 + 12,500 = 600,040

STEP 4 - Revenue change %:
   600,040 / 500,000 - 1 = 0.20008 = +20.0%
Result: 600,040, which is +20.0% over 500,000. Target hit, and only two segments (A and B) are orange, so the condition is satisfied.

Two important notes. (1) The solution is not unique. Many other price combinations also reach 600,000. For instance you could leave a segment un-orange and lift its price more, or split the increase differently across A and B. The grader is checking that you reach roughly +20% while obeying the two-orange limit, not that you found one exact answer. (2) A is the highest-leverage segment because it has the largest Number of Customers x Subscription Amount base (100 x 25,000 = 2,500,000), so each 0.01 you add to A's rate adds 25,000 of revenue (before the orange drop). A small nudge on A does more than a big push on D.

How to think about it under time pressure

You need an extra 100,000 (to go from 500,000 to 600,000). The fastest path:

  1. Find the gap: target minus current = 600,000 minus 500,000 = 100,000 of new revenue needed.
  2. Attack the high-V% segments first (A, then B). Each 0.01 added to A's rate is worth about 100 x 25,000 x 0.01 = 25,000 (or 23,750 if A is orange).
  3. If you make a segment orange, remember you first lose 5% of its current contribution, so price up enough to cover that loss plus your share of the 100,000 gap.
  4. Check the two-orange limit and add up M7. Adjust one rate to land on 600,000.

Long-answer questions

Q1. Explain why Value-Based Pricing is called the "holy grail", list its four advantages from the reading, and say why it fits digital products better than cost-plus.

A. It is called the holy grail because it is highly desirable but hard to achieve: it needs in-depth market research to understand how customers perceive value, plus a specific strategy to capture that value. Its four advantages are: (1) it focuses on customer value (charging what customers will pay, not what production cost); (2) it maximizes profitability by capturing a larger share of the value created, which is huge when marginal cost is near zero; (3) it gives flexibility, allowing different prices per segment based on perceived value (the reading cites Microsoft's education offering); and (4) it forces customer-centricity, a deep understanding of customer needs that leads to better products and experience. It beats cost-plus for digital products because cost-plus ties price to a near-zero cost (giving away most of the value), whereas value-based prices on the benefit delivered, letting software capture far more profit.

Q2. The reading warns against competing on price for digital products. Explain the "red ocean" problem and describe the alternative the reading recommends.

A. Because digital products have near-zero marginal cost, any competitor can keep cutting price toward zero. If everyone competes on price, all of them end up operating on extremely low margins. This bloody race to the bottom is the "red ocean". The reading's recommended alternative is to compete on uniqueness instead of price: solve a specific customer's problem far better than anyone else, deliver a superior user experience, or provide frictionless onboarding. In short, differentiate rather than discount.

Q3. Walk through the six-point conclusion checklist the reading gives for choosing a pricing strategy, and explain why "pricing is not set-and-forget".

A. The six points are: (1) understand your cost structure and how it changes as you grow; (2) understand what customers value, using market research, surveys, and interviews; (3) understand what customers are trying to achieve (their "jobs"); (4) select your primary strategy, cost leader versus differentiator; (5) be clear about your value proposition and what is unique; and (6) analyze competitors, including substitute products. Pricing is not set-and-forget because markets, competitors, costs, and customer perceptions all change. The reading says to test different approaches, regularly analyze how customers respond, and adjust when needed. Pricing is a strategic decision that greatly affects the business, so it is worth continually revisiting.

Q4. Several strategies in the reading combine. Give three combinations the reading names and explain the logic of each.

A. (1) Apple = Premium + Value-Based. A high (premium) price signals quality, and it is justified by genuine perceived value in design, quality, and user experience. (2) Tesla = Value-Based + Penetration. Tesla prices on the perceived value of an eco-friendly, high-end lifestyle and a future vision, while also lowering prices over time (penetration) to expand the electric-vehicle market and make EVs more affordable. (3) Loss Leader + ongoing monetization (Amazon Kindle, Gillette): sell the first item cheap or at a loss, then profit on follow-on digital sales, memberships, or replacement blades. A fourth valid combination is Price Discrimination (location-based) with Value-Based, charging each region or segment by the value it perceives.

Next Best Action IN SCOPE

This section explains Next Best Action from scratch, using the telco (telecommunications, the mobile-phone-network industry) case study from the in-class reading handout. You do not need any prior knowledge. By the end you should be able to define NBA, draw its four-tier architecture, define every technical term it uses, and recite the documented business results. The exercise in the very next section (the Base Exclusion Funnel) is the data-cleaning step that happens before an NBA campaign, so learn NBA first and the funnel will make far more sense.

Key term. NBA (Next Best Action). A customer-centric marketing paradigm (a way of thinking that puts the individual customer at the centre) that picks a series of highly targeted, prioritized actions for one customer or a group of customers. These actions are usually more than one, ranked by priority, and can be offers, services, or propositions (a proposition is a packaged value offer, for example "double your data for 30 days"). The customers are selected using a sophisticated mechanism that weighs two sides against each other: on one side the customer's demographics, profile, behavior, interests and needs, and on the other side the company's business objectives, policies and regulations.

The one sentence to memorize: the golden marketing principle

NBA is built on the golden marketing principle: deliver the "right offer to the right customer at the right time and through the right channel." Notice the four "rights." Each one maps loosely to a tier of the architecture below: the right customer (Tier 1, understanding), the right offer (Tier 2, deciding), the right channel and right time (Tier 3, placing and pitching), and a feedback loop that keeps all four correct over time (Tier 4, adaptive learning). If you can write this sentence and name the four tiers, you have the spine of the whole topic.

Why telcos needed this (the case-study motivation). The reading argues the cellular industry must reinvent itself, especially in South Asia. Voice services (ordinary phone calls), once the largest revenue contributor, were facing margin dilution (shrinking profit per rupee of sales) to below 50 percent, while 3G (third-generation mobile data) was set to push data services into the "driving seat." Fierce competition made customer retention (keeping existing customers from leaving) the biggest challenge. The prepaid base (customers who pay in advance, as opposed to monthly contract customers) is highly dynamic, so by the time slow analytics turned into a tactical response the market had already shifted. The fix is to become truly customer-centric and act proactively through micro-segmentation. That is the NBA promise.

The four-tier NBA architecture

The reading lays out NBA as a four-tier approach. Read the tiers as a pipeline (output of one feeds the next), with the fourth tier looping back to the first.

Tier 1. Understanding the customer

The goal is to know each subscriber (a subscriber is one mobile customer) as well as possible. Four ingredients are mixed together:

  • Strategic segmentation. Divide the entire subscriber base into broad lifecycle groups: new sales (just joined), growing (usage rising), mature (settled, steady usage), declining (usage falling, at risk of leaving), and reactivated (had left or gone quiet, now active again). These groups are based on lifecycle stage and certain conditions. This is the "big-picture" cut of the base.
  • Tactical segments. On top of the strategic groups, many finer segments are created using unsupervised clustering (defined below) on behavior and usage, built from a number of parameters or variables. Each tactical segment has a unique profile, different from every other tactical segment.
  • Propensity / prediction scores. Numeric scores from prediction models that estimate how likely (how "propense") a customer is to do something in the future, for example take an offer, churn (leave), or buy data. Defined in detail below.
  • Social parameters. The customer's social influence and social segment (for example, are they a hub that influences many others) add another dimension to the decision. Customer profiling is described as a continuously evolving process, so new dimensions keep getting added.

Tier 2. Deciding the most relevant offer

Now bring all of Tier 1's results together into a single view of each customer. Then apply supervised clustering using thresholds, conditions and ranges (decided after significant R and D, that is research and development) to break customers into smaller, more targeted groups called micro-segments.

  • The dormancy reference of 0 to 150 days. Dormancy means how long a subscriber has been inactive (not using the service). The standard industry practice is to take the "90-day active base" (only count customers active within the last 90 days). The reading deliberately widens this to 0 to 150 days, because a customer who has been quiet for 91 to 150 days still has vital chances of becoming active again. Keeping these subscribers in scope means there are offers ready for such potential reactivations. This is a memorable, examable detail: 0 to 150 days, not the standard 90, so that 91-to-150-day subscribers can be reactivated.
  • Pick a prioritized set of three offers. Once micro-segments are finalized, the micro-segmentation decides a set of three offers that best suit the general and specific characteristics and needs of each micro-segment, along with their priority order.

Tier 3. Placing and pitching the offer on channels

"Pitching" means presenting the offer to the customer. A "channel" is a route through which you reach the customer, and a "touch point" is any moment of contact. Offers are placed on all channels so they can be pitched at every touch point. The reading stresses that besides inbound channels (the customer contacts the company), the outbound channel (the company reaches out to the customer) is now encouraged. The listed channels are:

  • Contact Centers (phone support lines)
  • Service Centers (physical branches)
  • Outbound (the company proactively calls or messages the customer)
  • New Acquisition Activation (offers presented when a new customer is being signed up)
  • Service APP (a self-service mobile app, listed as "to be explored")
  • Other digital channels

Tier 4. Adaptive learning and self-healing

This is the feedback loop that makes NBA "powerful and resilient." It is based on analysing offer takers (customers who accepted an offer) versus non-offer takers / decliners (customers who refused).

  • Offer decliners are marked, filtered, and given a decline score (a number capturing how and how often they reject offers).
  • The decline score feeds back into the adaptive learning. The self-healing module filters decliners out while calculating the NBA offer set iteratively (repeatedly, refining each round), so the system stops wasting the same rejected offer on the same person.
  • The customer's rejection record for a given month is carried forward and taken into account when identifying the best offers for the next month.

This closing of the loop lets the system learn and incorporate changes as it gets ready for the next set of offers, which the reading frames as change management.

The technical terms, defined plainly

Key term. Strategic vs tactical segmentation. Strategic segmentation is the broad, slow-changing, lifecycle cut of the base (new / growing / mature / declining / reactivated). Tactical segmentation is the fine-grained, fast-changing cut built on behavior and usage that sits on top of the strategic groups. Memory hook: strategic = the big lifecycle buckets, tactical = the small behavior-driven micro-cuts used for the next move.
Key term. Unsupervised vs supervised clustering. "Clustering" means grouping similar customers together. Unsupervised clustering has no pre-set answer or label; the algorithm finds natural groupings in the data on its own (used to discover tactical segments). Supervised clustering uses known thresholds, conditions, and ranges that humans set after research, to deliberately split customers into defined groups (used to form micro-segments). Memory hook: unsupervised = "find groups for me," supervised = "split them by my rules."
Key term. Propensity score. A number (often 0 to 1, or a percentage) from a prediction model that says how likely a customer is to take a particular action in the future (accept an offer, churn, upgrade). Higher propensity means more likely. In the funnel exercise next door, the Model Decile column is a propensity-based ranking, and the top deciles are the highest-propensity customers.
Key term. Micro-segment. A small, tightly defined group of customers who share specific characteristics and needs, produced by supervised clustering with thresholds and ranges. Each micro-segment gets its own prioritized set of three offers. Micro-segments are smaller and more targeted than tactical segments.
Exam trap. Do not confuse the two segmentation pairs. There are two different "strategic vs tactical" and "supervised vs unsupervised" axes. Tactical segments are built with unsupervised clustering (discover groups), while micro-segments are built with supervised clustering (split by rules). A classic trick question swaps these. Also remember the dormancy number is 150 days (not 90) and the offer set is three offers.

The Base Exclusion Funnel IN SCOPE

This is the hardest Excel exercise in the course, so it is explained here in full, assuming zero prior knowledge of statistics. The Base Exclusion Funnel is a base-cleaning exercise: before a telco runs an NBA campaign (the topic above), it must clean its subscriber base, meaning remove bad rows (statistical outliers) and bad actors (fraud), while protecting its most valuable customers. The output is a trustworthy, high-value "Final Base" to aim offers at. We will define every term, walk the method step by step, do the arithmetic, and then explain a subtle statistical lesson that the exercise is secretly teaching.

Key term. Base. In telco language, the "base" is simply the full set of subscribers (customers). "Base cleaning" or "base exclusion" means filtering that set down to the subscribers you actually want to keep for a campaign. A "funnel" is a step-by-step narrowing: you start with everyone at the wide top and end with a smaller, cleaner group at the narrow bottom.

The dataset (Base Funnal Analysis Data)

There are 158,039 subscribers, one per row. (The raw file has 158,040 data rows because one row is a duplicate header artifact; the official base used in every calculation is 158,039.) Each row has these columns:

  • ID and Device ID: identifiers for the subscriber and their handset.
  • Vol KPI1 and Vol KPI2: two Volume KPIs. A KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is a measured number that tracks performance. "Volume" here means an amount, for example data volume or spend.
  • Freq KPI1 and Freq KPI2: two Frequency KPIs (how often something happens, for example number of recharges or calls).
  • A score (0 to about 100) for each of those four KPIs: Vol KPI1 score, Vol KPI2 score, Freq KPI1 score, Freq KPI2 score. Each raw KPI is converted to a 0-to-100-ish score so the four very differently-sized KPIs can be added together fairly. (Scoring puts everything on a common scale; this is called normalisation.)
  • Gaming Acc Flag: a fraud flag. 1 means the account is a fraud / gaming account (an account abusing the system, for example to farm incentives); 0 means clean.
  • Combined Score: the sum of the four KPI scores. Since each score tops out near 100, the Combined Score theoretically ranges 0 to 400. This single number summarises how valuable/engaged a subscriber is.
  • Model Decile: the propensity model's ranking, from 1 to 10. A "decile" splits the base into ten equal tenths. Here the decile expresses the propensity model's ordering of value/likelihood, and the top three deciles are treated as the most valuable subscribers.
  • Four flag columns to be computed (these start empty and you fill them): Qualify, Slide back, Retained Base, Final Base. A "flag" is a binary 1/0 marker.
Key term. KPI (Key Performance Indicator). A single measured number used to judge performance. This exercise has four (two Volume, two Frequency). Each is scored to roughly 0 to 100 and the four scores are summed into the Combined Score, which is the variable the whole funnel acts on.

Step a. The bell-curve / empirical-rule idea

What the empirical rule says

Many natural quantities follow a normal distribution, a symmetric bell-shaped curve. For a normal distribution the empirical rule (also called the 68-95-99.7 rule) states:

  • About 68% of values lie within mu plus or minus 1 sigma.
  • About 95% of values lie within mu plus or minus 2 sigma.
  • About 99.7% of values lie within mu plus or minus 3 sigma.

The intent of the exercise is to keep the "middle 95 percent" of subscribers (the normal, typical customers) and exclude the extreme roughly 5 percent of outliers, split across the two tails (the very low scorers and the very high scorers). An outlier is a value far from the rest. The instruction sheet literally says: "apply Bell Curve Ref (mu plus/minus 2 sigma) to retain 95% of the base."

Key term. Tail, Head, outlier. On a bell curve the two thin ends are the tails. The Tail (low end) holds the smallest scores; the Head (high end) holds the largest scores. Excluding outliers means cutting off subscribers in these extreme ends. In the funnel summary, "Head" counts high-score subs removed and "Tail" counts low-score subs removed.

Step b. The crucial teaching nuance (read this twice)

Exam trap (the heart of this exercise). Compute the literal mu plus/minus 2 sigma band first: 2 sigma = 2 times 103.12 = 206.24 (the exercise rounds to 206.25). So the band is 170.85 plus/minus 206.25, which is roughly [-35, 377]. But the actual Combined Score only ranges from 1 to 368. The band [-35, 377] is WIDER than the data range [1, 368], so a literal mu plus/minus 2 sigma rule would exclude NOBODY (every real score already sits inside [-35, 377]). Because this score is so spread out (sigma is large relative to the range), the textbook rule is useless here as written. The lesson: always sanity-check a statistical rule against the actual data range before trusting it.

What the exercise does instead: practical score limits

Because the statistical band catches nobody, the exercise switches to practical score limits that actually trim the extreme tails of the real data:

  • Lower limit of Score, about 20. Subscribers scoring at or below roughly 20 are too low (barely engaged, likely junk rows) and are excluded from the Tail.
  • Upper limit of Score, about 350. Subscribers scoring at or above roughly 350 are extreme high outliers and are excluded from the Head.

So the retained band becomes 20 < score < 350, chosen by judgment to clip the tails, not by the blind mu plus/minus 2 sigma formula. This is the bridge from the textbook to the real data.

Step c. The four binary flags (1 = yes, 0 = no)

Each subscriber row gets four computed flags. Compute them in this order, because later flags depend on earlier ones.

Flag 1: Qualify

Qualify = 1 if the score is greater than the lower limit AND less than the upper limit, that is 20 < score < 350. Otherwise Qualify = 0. In words: did this subscriber land in the retained middle band (not in either tail)? In Excel this is an AND condition, for example =IF(AND(Score>20, Score<350), 1, 0).

Flag 2: Slide back

Slide back = 1 if the customer is in the top three Model Deciles (the three deciles the propensity model ranks most valuable). Otherwise 0. "Sliding back" means rescuing a high-potential customer back into the base even if their Combined Score put them in a tail and failed Qualify. The business logic: the propensity model thinks this person is very valuable, so do not throw them away over a single score. In Excel, for example =IF(Decile>=8, 1, 0) if the top three deciles are 8, 9, 10 (the exact decile numbering depends on how the model orders them; the rule is simply "top 3 deciles").

Flag 3: Retained Base

Retained Base = 1 if (Qualify + Slide back) is non-zero, that is if the subscriber qualified OR was slid back (or both). Otherwise 0. This is a logical OR expressed as a sum: if either flag is 1, the total is at least 1, so the subscriber is kept. In Excel, for example =IF((Qualify+SlideBack)>0, 1, 0).

Flag 4: Final Base

Final Base = Retained Base, but only if the customer is NOT a fraudster. So Final Base = 1 only when Retained Base = 1 AND Gaming Acc Flag = 0. If the subscriber is fraud (Gaming flag = 1), Final Base = 0 no matter what. In Excel, for example =IF(AND(Retained=1, Gaming=0), 1, 0). Fraud is removed LAST, on purpose (explained below).

Exam trap. Retained Base uses OR (Qualify OR Slide back), implemented as "sum is non-zero," NOT AND. A common mistake is to require both. The whole point of slide-back is to rescue customers who FAILED Qualify, so requiring both would defeat it. And Final Base ANDs in "not fraud," so a high-value non-fraud customer who was slid back can still reach the Final Base, while a fraudster is dropped even if they qualified.

Q. Why is the retained figure 90.42% and not exactly 95%?

A. Because the Combined Score is NOT a clean, symmetric normal distribution. It is left-heavy (skewed), so the two tails are not equal. The low tail removed 6.00% but the high tail removed only 3.58%, totalling 9.58% excluded and leaving 90.42%. A perfectly normal distribution with a symmetric mu plus/minus 2 sigma cut would leave about 95% with roughly 2.5% in each tail. The real data is lopsided, so the kept fraction (90.42%) and the tail split (6.00% vs 3.58%) both differ from the textbook ideal. This is the same lesson as Step b: the data is not the textbook bell curve.

Q. Why does retention RISE after slide-back and then FALL after gaming removal?

A. Rise (Stage 1 to Stage 2, 90.42% up to 93.31%): slide-back is a rescue. It adds roughly 4,574 high-propensity subscribers (top three deciles) back into the retained set even though their score had excluded them. Adding people back can only increase retention. Fall (Stage 2 to Stage 3, 93.31% down to 89.11%): gaming removal is a subtraction. It removes the fraud-flagged subscribers, about 6,640 of whom were sitting in the retained set, so retention drops. In short: a rescue adds (retention up), a fraud purge subtracts (retention down).

Step e. Business purpose, and why fraud is removed last

What this funnel is for

This funnel cleans a subscriber base before an NBA campaign (the topic in the first section). It does three jobs in sequence: (1) remove statistical outliers using practical score limits, (2) protect high-propensity customers by sliding the valuable ones back in, and (3) strip out fraud / gaming accounts. The result is a Final Base that is trustworthy (no fraud) and high-value (outliers gone, but valuable customers preserved), so the NBA offers are aimed at the right people. This connects directly to NBA's golden principle of the "right customer."

Why fraud removal is applied LAST. Fraud is an absolute disqualifier: a gaming account must never receive an offer, regardless of its score or decile. By cleaning for score outliers first, then rescuing genuine high-value customers, and only THEN removing fraud, the funnel guarantees that no fraudster slips through on the strength of a good score or a top decile. If fraud were removed first, a fraudster could in principle be slid back in on a high decile later; doing it last makes the fraud filter the final, overriding gate. It also keeps the accounting clean: you can see how many of the already-retained subscribers turned out to be fraud (here 6,640 of the 147,467).
Exam trap. Final Base is not simply "Retained minus all 6,898 fraudsters." Only the fraudsters who were still in the retained set get subtracted (6,640 of them), because the other 258 fraud accounts had already been excluded by the score limits. So 147,467 minus 6,640 equals 140,827, not 147,467 minus 6,898. Watch this if asked to derive the final count.

  1. All 6,898 gaming-flagged accounts

  1. The average of the two Volume KPIs

Product Metrics & the Product Teardown IN SCOPE

A metric is just a number you measure on a regular basis so you can tell whether something is getting better or worse. In product management, metrics are how a Product Manager (PM, the person who decides what a product does and how it reaches customers) answers the single most important question in this whole course: "How do you know if your product is good?" You cannot improve what you do not measure, so this section is a clean reference to every metric in the reading, each explained in plain English, with its formula where one exists and one fintech (financial-technology, software for banking and money) example. Read it once for understanding, then use the big table at the end to revise.

Key term. KPI. KPI stands for Key Performance Indicator. It is a metric you have chosen as one of the few that matter most for your goal. Every KPI is a metric, but not every metric is important enough to be a KPI. When the reading says DAU "can be a revenue-generating activity or any other engagement KPI", it means you pick which user action counts as "active".
Key term. Cohort. A cohort is a group of users who share a starting point in time, usually the month or week they signed up. "The January cohort" means everyone who joined in January. Cohorts let you compare apples to apples: you watch how the January joiners behave over time separately from the February joiners, instead of mixing everyone together. Several metrics below are measured "by cohort".

Acquisition metrics: what does a customer cost to get?

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

Plain English. The total cost of acquiring one new customer. You add up everything you spent to win customers (advertising, sales salaries, referral bonuses, marketing tools) over a period, then divide by the number of new customers you actually got.

Formula. CAC = Total acquisition spend in a period / Number of new customers acquired in that period.

Fintech example. A digital wallet spends 5,000,000 PKR on ads and field agents in a month and signs up 10,000 new wallet users. CAC = 5,000,000 / 10,000 = 500 PKR per new user. If the average user is worth less than 500 PKR to the business, the company is losing money on every sign-up.

CAC by Source

Plain English. The same CAC, but broken down per marketing channel (each route by which a customer found you: TikTok ads, Google search, referrals, retail agents). It tells you which channel is cheap and which is expensive, so you spend more on the efficient ones.

Formula. CAC for a source = Spend on that source / New customers from that source.

Fintech example. The wallet spent 2,000,000 PKR on TikTok (4,000 users, CAC = 500) and 3,000,000 PKR on retail agents (6,000 users, CAC = 500). If next month TikTok's CAC jumps to 900 while agents stay at 500, you shift budget to agents.

Conversion Rate, and Conversion Rate by Source

Plain English. Conversion Rate is the percentage of people who take a desired action (turning from a mere visitor into a customer, or completing any other key goal you set, like making a first deposit). Conversion Rate by Source splits that same percentage by channel, showing which channel sends visitors that actually convert, not just visitors who bounce.

Formula. Conversion Rate = (Number who took the desired action / Number of people who had the chance to) x 100.

Fintech example. 50,000 people open the wallet's sign-up page; 8,000 finish and fund the account. Conversion Rate = 8,000 / 50,000 = 16%. If Google visitors convert at 22% but TikTok visitors convert at 9%, Google sends higher-intent traffic even if TikTok sends more raw clicks.

Onboarding Completion Rate by Cohort

Plain English. Onboarding is the first-time setup a new user goes through (verify identity, link a bank, make a first transaction). This metric is the percentage of a given cohort who finish that setup. Tracking it by cohort shows whether changes you made to onboarding actually helped newer joiners.

Formula. Onboarding Completion Rate = (Users in the cohort who completed onboarding / Total users in the cohort) x 100.

Fintech example. Of 1,000 users in the March cohort, 620 finished KYC (Know Your Customer, the legal identity check) and made a first transaction. Rate = 62%. After you simplify the KYC screen, the April cohort hits 78%, proving the fix worked.

Engagement metrics: how much are people using it?

Daily Active Users (DAU) and Monthly Active Users (MAU)

Plain English. DAU is the number of unique users who engage with the product on a given day; MAU is the number of unique users who engage at least once in a month. "Unique" means each person is counted once no matter how many times they open the app. You decide what "active" means (opening the app, or a revenue action like a payment).

Formula. These are counts, not ratios: DAU = count of distinct users active that day; MAU = count of distinct users active that month.

Fintech example. A payments app has 200,000 people who make or receive at least one payment on Monday (DAU = 200,000) and 1,000,000 distinct people who transact at least once in the month (MAU = 1,000,000).

Stickiness Ratio (= DAU / MAU)

Plain English. Stickiness measures how often your monthly users come back within the month, which signals how much the product has become a habit. It is defined as DAU divided by MAU. A high ratio means the average monthly user shows up on many days; a low ratio means they visit once a month and forget you.

Formula. Stickiness = DAU / MAU (often shown as a percentage). A common reading: a ratio of 0.20 means the average user is active on about 6 of 30 days.

Fintech example. DAU = 200,000 and MAU = 1,000,000, so Stickiness = 200,000 / 1,000,000 = 0.20 = 20%. A daily-use app like a wallet wants this high; a once-a-month bill app naturally runs lower.

Session Duration and Frequency

Plain English. A "session" is one continuous visit to the app. Session Duration is the average length of a session; Session Frequency is how often sessions happen (sessions per user per day or week). Together they describe whether people dip in briefly and often, or stay long but rarely.

Formula. Average Session Duration = Total time across all sessions / Number of sessions. Frequency = Number of sessions / Number of users (over a period).

Fintech example. A budgeting app sees average sessions of 3 minutes, 5 times a week. A short, frequent pattern is healthy for a check-your-balance product; a tax-filing app would instead see long but rare sessions.

User Actions per Session

Plain English. The average number of meaningful actions a user takes in one session (taps, transfers, screens viewed). It shows depth of engagement: are people doing a lot once they are in, or opening and leaving?

Formula. User Actions per Session = Total actions / Total sessions.

Fintech example. In one session a wallet user checks balance, pays a bill, and sends money to a friend = 3 actions. If the average is 1.1 actions per session, most people open the app and bounce, a warning sign.

Feature metrics: is the new thing being used and loved?

Feature Adoption Rate

Plain English. The percentage of users who start using a new feature within a set time after it launches. It tells you whether a feature you built was worth building.

Formula. Feature Adoption Rate = (Users who used the feature / Total eligible users) x 100, within a chosen window.

Fintech example. The wallet launches "split a bill with friends". In 30 days, 90,000 of 1,000,000 users try it. Adoption = 9%. Low adoption may mean it is hard to find or solves a problem few users have.

Feature Fit Index (FFI)

Plain English. The percentage of users who say they would be disappointed if a particular feature were removed. It measures how essential a feature feels, not just whether it gets clicked. A feature can have low adoption yet very high FFI among the few who rely on it (and vice versa).

Formula. FFI = (Users who would be disappointed if the feature were removed / Users surveyed) x 100. You collect this with a survey question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use [feature]?"

Fintech example. You survey users of the auto-savings feature; 68% say they would be "very disappointed" if it disappeared. FFI = 68%, a strong sign the feature is core and should be protected and promoted.

Exam trap. Do not confuse Feature Adoption Rate with Feature Fit Index. Adoption = how many people started using it. FFI = how many would be disappointed if it were removed. A feature can have high adoption but low FFI (people click it but would not miss it), or low adoption but high FFI (a small group depends on it heavily). Both numbers together tell the real story.

Retention and churn: do people stay?

Churn Rate

Plain English. The percentage of customers who stop using the product (or cancel their subscription) over a given period. High churn means you are filling a leaky bucket: you keep pouring in new users but they drain out the bottom.

Formula. Churn Rate = (Customers lost during the period / Customers at the start of the period) x 100.

Fintech example. A lending app starts the month with 50,000 active borrowers and 2,500 stop using it. Monthly churn = 2,500 / 50,000 = 5%.

Retention Rate

Plain English. The mirror image of churn: the percentage of customers who keep using the product over a period. Retention + Churn = 100% for the same group and period.

Formula. Retention Rate = (Customers still active at the end / Customers at the start) x 100. In the example above, retention = 95%.

Fintech example. If 95% of a month's borrowers are still active at month-end, monthly retention is 95%. Small differences compound: 95% monthly retention keeps about 54% of users after a year, while 90% keeps only about 28%.

Cohort Retention

Plain English. Retention measured separately for each cohort, tracked over time. You line up "% of the January cohort still active in month 1, month 2, month 3..." so you can see exactly when users drop off and whether newer cohorts retain better than older ones.

Formula. For cohort C in period n: Cohort Retention = (Members of C active in period n / Original size of C) x 100.

Fintech example. The January wallet cohort: 100% in month 0, 70% in month 1, 55% in month 2. If the April cohort holds 65% in month 2 after a redesign, retention is improving.

Revenue metrics: how much money does it make?

Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) and Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR)

Plain English. "Recurring" revenue is predictable, repeating income, typically from subscriptions. MRR is the total expected revenue from all active subscriptions in a month; ARR is the same idea over a year, used for long-term planning.

Formula. MRR = Sum of all active monthly subscription fees. ARR = MRR x 12 (or the sum of annual contract values).

Fintech example. A premium-banking tier has 10,000 subscribers paying 300 PKR/month. MRR = 10,000 x 300 = 3,000,000 PKR. ARR = 3,000,000 x 12 = 36,000,000 PKR.

Reactivation MRR

Plain English. The monthly recurring revenue you win back from customers who had churned (left) and then came back. It isolates the money generated specifically by win-back efforts, separate from brand-new customers.

Formula. Reactivation MRR = Sum of monthly fees from previously churned customers who reactivated this period.

Fintech example. 500 lapsed premium users return after a "come back, first month half price" campaign and resume paying 300 PKR/month. Reactivation MRR = 500 x 300 = 150,000 PKR.

Average Revenue Per User (ARPU) and Average Revenue Per Active User (ARPAU)

Plain English. ARPU is the average revenue per user over a period, dividing by all users (including dormant ones). ARPAU divides the same revenue by active users only. ARPAU is always higher than ARPU when you have dormant users, because the denominator is smaller. The gap between them tells you how much dead weight (inactive sign-ups) is dragging down your average.

Formula. ARPU = Total revenue in period / Total users. ARPAU = Total revenue in period / Active users in period.

Fintech example. Revenue this month = 10,000,000 PKR. Total users = 1,000,000, but only 600,000 were active (MAU). ARPU = 10,000,000 / 1,000,000 = 10 PKR. ARPAU = 10,000,000 / 600,000 = 16.67 PKR. The active users are each worth far more than the all-user average suggests.

Exam trap. ARPU divides by ALL users; ARPAU divides by ACTIVE users only. Because active users are fewer, ARPAU is the larger number. If a question gives you total revenue, total users, and active users, make sure you pick the right denominator. Mixing them up is a classic mistake.

Customer Lifetime Value (CLTV, also LTV)

Plain English. The total revenue you expect from one customer across the entire time they stay with you. It answers "how much is a customer worth in total?" You compare it against CAC: if it costs more to acquire a customer than they are ever worth, the business does not work.

Formula (simple version). CLTV = ARPU x average customer lifetime, and the average lifetime (in the same time units as ARPU) = 1 / churn rate. So CLTV = ARPU / churn rate.

Why lifetime = 1 / churn. If 5% of customers leave each month (churn = 0.05), the average customer stays 1 / 0.05 = 20 months. The smaller the churn, the longer they stay.

Fintech example. Monthly ARPU = 10 PKR and monthly churn = 5% (0.05). Average lifetime = 1 / 0.05 = 20 months. CLTV = 10 x 20 = 200 PKR. If CAC is 500 PKR, you lose money; you must either cut CAC, raise ARPU, or reduce churn.

Key term. The CAC-to-LTV relationship. A healthy business has CLTV comfortably above CAC (a common rule of thumb is LTV at least 3x CAC). "CAC payback period" is a related idea: how many months of ARPU it takes to earn back the CAC. CAC payback = CAC / ARPU per month. With CAC = 500 and monthly ARPU = 10, payback = 50 months, which is far too long.

Referral, satisfaction, and support metrics

Referral Rate and Member-get-Member (MGM)

Plain English. Referral Rate is the percentage of users who refer the product to new people. MGM (Member-get-Member) is a specific kind of referral campaign where both the referrer (existing member) and the referee (the friend they bring) receive a reward, so both sides are motivated. MGM is a cheap acquisition channel because your own happy users do the marketing.

Formula. Referral Rate = (Users who referred at least one person / Total users) x 100.

Fintech example. The wallet offers "invite a friend, you both get 200 PKR once they make a first payment". 120,000 of 1,000,000 users send at least one invite, so Referral Rate = 12%. Because the reward only pays out on a funded first payment, the CAC stays controlled.

Net Promoter Score (NPS)

Plain English. NPS measures loyalty by asking one question: "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend us to a friend?" Respondents are grouped as Promoters (9 to 10, loyal fans), Passives (7 to 8, satisfied but unenthusiastic), and Detractors (0 to 6, unhappy and may spread bad word of mouth). NPS is the percentage of Promoters minus the percentage of Detractors. Passives are counted in the total but cancel out of the subtraction.

Formula. NPS = % Promoters minus % Detractors. The result ranges from minus 100 (everyone a detractor) to plus 100 (everyone a promoter). Anything positive means more fans than critics.

Fintech example. Of 1,000 surveyed wallet users: 500 are promoters (50%), 300 passives (30%), 200 detractors (20%). NPS = 50 minus 20 = +30. Decent, but you want to convert passives into promoters to push higher.

Exam trap. NPS uses a 0 to 10 scale, but the buckets are uneven: 9-10 promoters, 7-8 passives, 0-6 detractors. A "6" is a detractor, not a passive. And the formula ignores passives in the subtraction (they only affect the percentages). NPS can be negative.

Customer Satisfaction Score (CSAT)

Plain English. A direct feedback score reflecting how satisfied customers are with the product or a specific interaction (often asked right after an event, like "How satisfied were you with this transaction?" on a 1 to 5 scale). Unlike NPS, which measures loyalty and likelihood to recommend, CSAT measures immediate satisfaction with a specific thing.

Formula. CSAT = (Number of satisfied responses, for example 4s and 5s on a 5-point scale / Total responses) x 100.

Fintech example. After a support chat, 850 of 1,000 users rate it 4 or 5 out of 5. CSAT = 85%.

Number of Support Tickets

Plain English. The raw volume of customer support requests raised over a period. A rising count flags areas that confuse or frustrate users (and points to where the product needs fixing). It is also a cost driver, since each ticket takes staff time.

Formula. A simple count: number of tickets opened in the period. Useful derived views: tickets per 1,000 users, or tickets by category.

Fintech example. After a new login screen ships, "cannot log in" tickets jump from 300 to 1,200 a week. The spike tells you the redesign broke something, even before other metrics react.

The Product Teardown

What a Product Teardown is

A Product Teardown is an in-depth analysis of a product's components, features, design, and strategy. Product managers and teams examine a product (their own or a competitor's) to uncover insights that reveal its strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities for enhancement and optimization. Think of it as taking the product apart piece by piece, like a mechanic stripping an engine, to understand exactly how and why it works (or fails). It is the structured way to answer "how do you know if your product is good?" for any product, not just by numbers but by reasoning through every dimension.

The reading groups teardowns into four families, each with its own sub-types. Memorize the four families and what lives under each.

How this ties to the course theme. The whole point of metrics and teardowns is to answer the course's recurring second question: "How do you know if your product is good?" Metrics give you the numeric evidence (is engagement up, is churn down, is CLTV above CAC?). The teardown gives you the structured reasoning (is the onboarding smooth, is the pricing sound, is it secure, how does it beat competitors?). A strong PM uses both: numbers to know what is happening, and a teardown to understand why and what to fix. If you cannot point to the metrics that prove your product is good, and cannot tear it down to explain its strengths and gaps, then you do not actually know whether it is good.

  2. 0.25 (25%); the average monthly user is active on about 1 in 4 days

Long-answer questions

Q1. Explain the difference between ARPU and ARPAU, why ARPAU is always at least as large, and what a big gap between them tells a PM.

A. ARPU (Average Revenue Per User) divides total revenue by every user, including dormant ones who generate nothing. ARPAU (Average Revenue Per Active User) divides the same revenue only by users who were active in the period. Because the active count is smaller than or equal to the total count, ARPAU is always greater than or equal to ARPU. A large gap means a big chunk of the user base is inactive: you have many sign-ups who do nothing, dragging the all-user average down. For a PM this signals an activation or retention problem. The product earns well from the people who actually use it (high ARPAU), but too few users reach that active state (low ARPU), so the fix is to convert dormant users into active ones, not necessarily to raise prices.

Q2. Define a Product Teardown and list the four families with at least one sub-type each. Then explain how teardowns and metrics together answer "how do you know if your product is good?"

A. A Product Teardown is an in-depth analysis of a product's components, features, design, and strategy, done to reveal strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities for improvement. The four families are: (1) User Experience & Engagement (UX, Onboarding, Conversion, Accessibility & Inclusivity); (2) Business & Growth Strategy (Business Model & Pricing, Growth & Acquisition, Customer Support & Retention, Competitive Analysis); (3) Product Functionality & Technical Aspects (Feature-specific, Technical & Architectural, Security & Performance); and (4) Retrospective & Evolution (Retrospective). Metrics answer the "good?" question with evidence (engagement, churn, CLTV vs CAC, NPS), telling you what is happening. The teardown answers it with structured reasoning across every dimension, telling you why it is happening and where to improve. Used together, the numbers detect a problem and the teardown diagnoses and prioritizes the fix.

Q3. Why can a feature have low Feature Adoption Rate but high Feature Fit Index, and how should a PM treat such a feature?

A. Adoption counts how many users start using the feature; FFI counts how many would be disappointed if it were removed. A feature used by only a small group (low adoption) can still be essential to that group, so among the people who rely on it the would-be-disappointed share is high (high FFI). An example is an auto-savings rule that only 9% of users set up, but 68% of those who use it say they would be very disappointed to lose it. A PM should not kill such a feature just because adoption looks low. Instead, treat it as a core feature for a valuable segment: protect it, and try to grow adoption (better discovery, clearer onboarding) so more users reach the group that loves it.

Gamification Case: FLYG (Financial Literacy for Youth via Gamification) IN SCOPE

This section explains a case study called FLYG: Financial Literacy for Youth via Gamification. It was a class simulation built by a team of 10 members from 7 different countries across Asia, so it is presented as an illustration of how to design a product for the service industry. We explain it from scratch: what the product is, who it is for, why it exists, how it makes money, and why "gamification" is the engine that makes it work.

Key term. Gamification. Gamification means adding game-like elements (points, levels, avatars, leaderboards, rewards, challenges) to something that is not normally a game, in order to make it more fun and engaging. FLYG applies gamification to financial education, which is normally dry, so that young people actually want to learn about money. The bet is that if learning feels like playing, people come back, form habits, and stick around.
Key term. Financial literacy. Financial literacy is the ability to understand and use money skills: budgeting (planning how to spend income), saving, investing (putting money to work to grow it), and managing loans and debt. The case argues that most youth in Asia have had little or no financial education, so they are "clueless about managing their income and funds". FLYG exists to close that gap.

Why this product, and why this segment (youth 15 to 24)

The problem and the opportunity

The case targets youth aged 15 to 24 (the United Nations definition of "youth"). The reasoning, built from a study of 7 Asian countries, has four parts:

  • A huge segment. There are about 1.1 billion youth worldwide aged 15 to 24, and over 60% of the world's youth live in Asia-Pacific. The case cites roughly 718 million youth in Asia. That is an enormous addressable market.
  • A real financial-literacy gap. Mobile and internet access is rising and financial inclusion is improving, but financial literacy is still sorely lacking. Most youth have had no financial education. Urban youth often rely fully on parents to manage money; rural youth face a lack of funds and income opportunities. The per-country evidence is stark (for example, only 9% of Hong Kong teenagers keep a detailed expense record, and 40% of Malaysian millennials spend beyond their means).
  • A massive gaming market to ride on. The global gaming industry was around 72.2 billion USD and projected to reach 174 billion USD by 2021, dominated by Asia-Pacific (China, Japan, South Korea lead). Youth already love games, so meeting them through gaming is natural.
  • Low e-wallet penetration = room to grow. E-wallet (electronic wallet, money stored and spent via an app) penetration was under 50% in the region (Malaysia 40%, Philippines 36%, Thailand 27%, Singapore 26%, others lower). That is huge headroom to bring youth onto a financial platform.

Key term. Financial inclusion. Financial inclusion means giving people access to useful, affordable financial services (a bank account, payments, savings, credit, insurance). Many youth, especially rural, are "unbanked" (no bank account) or "underbanked" (very limited access). FLYG aims to pull them into the formal financial system.

The mission, in one line. Create financial literacy and set a good foundation for responsible money management, in a format that appeals to Gen Z (born 1997 to 2012) and Millennials (born 1981 to 1996). The solution: teach youth how to manage money and use financial tools through a fun, accessible, and relevant platform.

The three pillars: PLAY, LEARN, EARN

FLYG is built on three pillars. The clever design is that the same platform does all three, and your activity in one pillar feeds the others (for example, learning and saving build a credit profile that unlocks real financial services).

Pillar 1: PLAY (make it fun and social)

The goal is engagement through play and social connection.

  • A gamified universe: the platform is a virtual world with multiple cities, shops, a university, a game arcade, and mini-games you can relate to (for example, learn budgeting in a "math class" mini-game, or stocks and bonds in a "science class").
  • Socialization via avatars: you build an in-app avatar (a digital character that represents you) and connect, communicate, and compete with friends and peers.
  • Points-based rewards: you earn reward points for completing learning modules and hitting financial goals.
  • A rewards marketplace: you spend or swap reward points (or game credits) for physical items such as gadgets and mobile phones, including on installment or BNPL (Buy Now Pay Later, where you receive the item now and pay in pieces over time).

The two example mini-games in the case are Payback (a scenario game about managing student debt without taking on too much) and Spent (a scenario game about surviving on a low income by budgeting). Scenarios update every quarter so players keep coming back.

Pillar 2: LEARN (interesting and interactive learning)

  • eLearning and certification: finance courses offered in partnership with education institutes or financial service providers, with professional certificates from universities or educational bodies.
  • Leaderboards: a ranking board that highlights your achievements and lets you share them with friends and family (social proof drives motivation).
  • Relevant, age-tiered syllabus: content designed by age group, language, and interest (even crypto), delivered as fun games, videos, podcasts, live sessions, quizzes, competitions, and challenges, with free introductory and intermediate courses and rewards for completing them.
  • Social inclusion: crowdfunding, donations, and scholarships for the needy, building a sense of community.

The learning tiers progress Basic to Intermediate to Advance to Professional, ending with a meaningful credit rating score tied to incentives, preparing youth for the real world.

Pillar 3: EARN (multiple ways to earn income and rewards)

The EARN pillar has three named services, each prefixed with a lowercase "e" for electronic:

  • eWallet: offers saving interest, fixed deposits, budgeting tools, and basic or low-risk investing modules, plus the ability to receive and send money to and from bank accounts and via P2P (peer-to-peer, sending money directly person to person).
  • eWork: freelance and online jobs to earn money (tutoring, designing, translating, or chores set by parents).
  • eScore: building a credit profile so that youth can access basic or starter credit services. A credit profile is a record of how reliably you handle money and repay, which lenders use to decide whether to lend to you.

The unifying idea: activities, savings, and engagement on the platform build a credit profile for the user, which unleashes more advanced financial services over time. PLAY draws them in, LEARN builds skills, EARN turns those skills into income and a credit identity.

Exam trap. Keep the three pillars and their services straight. PLAY = gamified city, avatars, mini-games, and the rewards marketplace. LEARN = eLearning, certification, leaderboards, age-tiered syllabus. EARN = eWallet, eWork, eScore. A common mistake is to put the rewards marketplace under EARN; in this case it sits under PLAY (you spend points there). The three "e" services (eWallet, eWork, eScore) are the EARN pillar.

The four-tier objectives and gains

What the product is trying to achieve

The case frames four objectives that the product delivers against:

  • Affordability: offer the lowest possible rates to obtain rewards, so the proposition stays relevant to every value segment (rich or poor youth).
  • Inclusion & Education: educational and "edutainment" tools, cashback channels, and delivery options help form the digital IDs of users, while encouraging disciplined payment behavior directly and indirectly.
  • Credit Risk Data Pipeline: the platform becomes a goldmine of data for future credit-risk modeling, capturing actual behavior (on-time vs delayed payments, full vs partial vs non-payment). This data lets the company judge who is a safe borrower.
  • Bundle Benefits: a package of perks to attract and retain users: free delivery, free top-ups, free transfers, free e-book vouchers, Member-get-Member discounts, family discounts, mobile-internet discounts, and surprise gifts.

Key term. Credit-risk modeling. This is using data about a person's past behavior to predict how likely they are to repay a loan. FLYG's edge is that even before a user borrows, their saving, learning, and payment activity quietly builds the data needed to assess that risk, which is valuable to lenders and to FLYG itself.

The business model: how FLYG makes money

Four revenue and cost components

  • Freemium. Freemium = "free" plus "premium": the core product is free to attract a large user base, and you charge for premium extras. In FLYG: unlock features with a subscription (with starting gifts and reward points on QR-code-led subscriptions), offer multiple subscription tiers for all levels of affordability, and charge pay-per-use for special financial classes, certification, or personal coaching.
  • Strategic Partnerships. Collaborate across the financial ecosystem (financial institutions, digital banks, e-wallet players, any fintech, universities, job portals) that want access to this youth user group. Revenue is shared from certification by education institutes, e-commerce providers, loan interest, job portals, and advertisement. NGO (Non-Governmental Organization, a non-profit body) grants, sponsorships, and scholarships fund the social-contribution side.
  • Interest from Trust Account. Earn interest from the balances held in user wallets and savings accounts. Interest earned from this trust account can be plowed back to help underserved communities on the platform.
  • Cost Structure. The costs to run it: platform dev-ops, IT infrastructure (including a Data Warehouse, a DWH, a big store of data for analytics, plus servers and hardware), proprietary software licenses, human resources (staff payroll), operational running costs and bills, facilities (office and equipment), marketing, research and consulting, insurance, and taxes.

Key term. Freemium (memorize). A model where the basic product is free to build a large audience, and a smaller fraction of users pay for premium features. It works well for digital products because the cost of serving one extra free user is tiny, so you can afford many free users while monetizing the few who upgrade.

Competitive analysis: why no one else does this

The gap FLYG fills

There are many apps targeting youth in Asia, but the case argues they are all silo in nature (each does only one thing in its own isolated bucket):

  • PLAY rivals: games like PUBG (over 70 million players) and Roblox (220 million monthly active players) are hugely popular and social, but purely entertainment. Finance-flavored games tend to be monotonous.
  • LEARN rivals: educational sites like Udemy, LinkedIn Learning, and Coursera are limited in social interaction and focus only on learning.
  • EARN rivals: UOB's TMRW app has gamification but limited to banking only; e-wallets are mass-market and skewed toward payments; standalone apps offer freelance jobs, financial planning, or robo-advisors separately.

Crucially, none combines all three with a marketplace powered by reward points, and their construct does not appeal to Gen Z and Millennials. FLYG's differentiation is the integrated PLAY + LEARN + EARN experience with a rewards marketplace, which no single competitor offers.

Why integration is the moat. A "moat" is a durable competitive advantage that protects you from rivals. FLYG's moat is that the three pillars reinforce each other: playing earns points, learning builds skills and a credit score, earning turns skills into income, and the credit data from all of it unlocks financial services. A pure game, a pure course site, or a pure wallet cannot replicate this loop, because they only own one piece.

Go-to-market strategy

How FLYG reaches youth

Key term. Go-to-market (GTM). Your plan for how you will reach customers and get them to adopt the product (which channels, messages, and tactics).

  • Mobile-first and social-media-led: since the audience lives on their phones, social media is the key channel: Instagram, YouTube, Facebook, TikTok, Snapchat, Discord, Tumblr.
  • Micro-influencers and ambassadors: work with smaller, trusted online personalities (micro-influencers) and brand ambassadors who feel authentic to youth, rather than only expensive celebrities.
  • FOMO and lifestyle tone: messaging is Authentic, Lifestyle-focused, and built to create FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out, the anxiety that others are enjoying something you are not, which pushes people to join). Focus on lifestyle, social, and experience, not on dry finance.
  • Omni-channel marketing: "omni-channel" means a consistent presence across every channel at once: social media, content marketing, SEO and SEM (Search Engine Optimization and Search Engine Marketing, ranking and advertising on search engines), digital ads, email, and partner-led BTL campaigns.

The funnel goals progress: Downloads to Registration to Interact to Transact (get them to install, sign up, engage, and finally transact, which is where money is made).

Regulations, risks, and challenges

Why a youth fintech super-app is hard to launch

Targeting minors with money across many countries creates serious constraints:

  • ID availability and KYC. KYC (Know Your Customer) is the legal duty to verify a customer's identity before onboarding. ID-card issuance ages range from birth to 18 across countries, so a youth may not have an ID to verify, blocking proper onboarding.
  • Bank-account age limits and parental consent. Opening a bank account may require parental consent, and in some countries accounts are only available at 17 and above. This creates disparity: the product cannot fully include the youngest youth, undermining the inclusion goal.
  • SIM-card age limits. Many telcos allow SIM registration only for those over 17 (in Pakistan the minimum is 18), so younger users must rely on a parent's supplementary SIM. Smartphone and internet penetration sits around 80 to 85% in most countries.
  • Minimum working age. The legal age to work averages around 15 (with limits on hours and type of work), which constrains the eWork earning feature for the youngest users.
  • Cross-border regulation. A financial super-app across borders must comply with many regulatory regimes that differ country to country.
  • Cultural and religious barriers. Rural youth (especially females in South Asia) may not have mobile or internet connections in their own names or be allowed social media. Muslim-majority countries prefer Shariah-compliant financial services (Shariah = Islamic law, which forbids interest-based and gambling products), so FLYG must avoid gambling and offer compliant finance.

The end-to-end system stages and the football analogy

The six stages a user (and the product) moves through

The case describes an end-to-end system. The six operational stages are:

  1. Customer Acquisition: reaching and signing up users via omni-channel interfacing and marketing responses (ATL/BTL/other campaigns). (ATL = Above The Line, broad mass-media advertising; BTL = Below The Line, targeted direct channels and partnerships.)
  2. Eligibility Assessment: checking criteria (age, SIM, digital and photo IDs, parental access) and running fraud detection (identity checks, catching repeat offenders).
  3. Locking & Delivery: for marketplace purchases, managing supply chain, stock, and delivery (and "locking" the product or installment plan to the user).
  4. Collection Management: collecting installment payments, with an evolving strategy (pricing, rewards, regulatory) and a collection strategy (in-house or outsourced), plus efficiency measures (delinquency plans for late payers, contact prioritization). "Delinquency" means falling behind on payments.
  5. Customer Experience / Value Management (CVM): measuring satisfaction via surveys and NPS, and running campaigns (cross-sell, up-sell, top-up, MGM). CVM means actively managing the value a customer gets and gives over their lifetime.
  6. Analytics & Risk Pipeline: monitoring, data collection, and analytics that feed the credit-risk models (the goldmine of behavioral data).

Key term. Cross-sell vs up-sell. Cross-sell = offering a different, complementary product (a user with a wallet is offered insurance). Up-sell = offering a bigger or premium version of what they already have (moving from a free tier to a paid subscription).

The football-team analogy (likely exam line). The case compares the six stages to a football (soccer) team: "until and unless all players multi-touch and team-play, getting a goal is impossible." The point is that no single stage can succeed alone. Acquisition without eligibility checks brings fraud; delivery without collection loses money; analytics without acquisition has no data. Only when every stage passes the ball to the next (team play) does the product "score a goal" (a satisfied, paying, low-risk customer). It is a vivid way to argue that the system must work end-to-end, not in isolated parts.

Per-country appendix (low yield, one sentence)

The appendix compiles one researcher's country profile each for Indonesia, Philippines, Malaysia, Pakistan, South Korea, Thailand, and Vietnam, showing that across all seven the same pattern holds: rising connectivity but weak youth financial literacy, varied bank-account and SIM age rules, and cultural or infrastructure barriers, which together justify a single gamified, inclusive platform.

Why gamification works for engagement and habit formation

The core insight. Financial education is normally boring, so people avoid it and never build the habit. Gamification fixes this with well-known behavioral hooks: (1) points and rewards give instant positive feedback, which trains people to repeat the behavior; (2) levels and progression (Basic to Professional) create a sense of advancement that pulls users forward; (3) leaderboards and avatars add social competition and identity, so learning becomes something you show off; (4) fresh content (scenarios that update quarterly) gives a reason to return; and (5) real rewards (the marketplace, top-100 prizes) connect virtual effort to tangible payoff. The result is a habit loop: a cue (notification or challenge), a routine (play or learn), and a reward (points, status, prizes), repeated until managing money becomes second nature. That habit is exactly what a financial-literacy product needs, because literacy comes from repeated practice, not a one-time lecture.

  3. Assumptions. The things you are taking as true for the plan to work (for example, that users have smartphones, or that a minimum investment threshold is acceptable to them).
  4. Product Definition & Eligibility. What the product is and the rules for each of the three variants (Basic, Intermediate, Advance), including eligibility criteria.
  5. Services Offered with benefits. The specific features and the benefit each delivers to the customer.
  6. Target Audience and segments. Who the customers are, split into segments (for example, street vendors, gig workers, small business owners).
  7. Value Proposition Canvas (customer journeys). The mapping of customer needs to the value you offer, linked to the variants in part 4. (See the key term below.)
  8. Financials. Cost, pricing, and profitability structures (the numeric model worked below).
  9. Scale Plans. How you will grow, initially, followed by a CVM (Customer Value Management) plan in a later week.

Key term. Value Proposition Canvas. A simple framework that puts the customer's "jobs" (what they are trying to get done), "pains" (frustrations), and "gains" (desired benefits) on one side, and your product's "pain relievers" and "gain creators" on the other, then checks that the two sides match. A "customer journey" is the step-by-step path a user takes from first hearing about you to becoming a loyal user. CVM (Customer Value Management) is the ongoing practice of maximizing the value a customer gives and gets over their lifetime (through cross-sell, up-sell, retention, and win-back).

The financial model, line by line (likely numeric exam question)

The worksheet gives a worked profitability illustration for the Basic tier. We rebuild it from scratch so you can reproduce or solve a variant in the exam. Every line shows the plain-English meaning, the formula, and the arithmetic for Year 1 and Year 2.

The Cohort is the group of subscribers in the Basic tier (34,493 people). The Invested Amount is the total money they have collectively deposited and that stays invested. The rates are the levers that turn deposits into profit for both sides.

Line 1: Invested per subscriber

Plain English. On average, how much has each subscriber invested? Divide the total invested amount by the number of subscribers.

Invested per subscriber = Invested Amount / Cohort

Year 1: 56,972,810 / 34,493 = 1,651.72
Year 2: 64,926,182 / 34,493 = 1,882.30

Year-on-year change = (1,882.30 / 1,651.72) - 1
                    = 0.1396 = +13.96%

Each subscriber invests about 1,651.72 in Year 1, rising 13.96% to 1,882.30 in Year 2 (the same people invest more as they trust the product).

Line 2: Organization's monthly share from re-investment (per subscriber)

Plain English. The organization re-invests the pooled money and earns an annual rate (13.5% in Year 1). To find the organization's monthly earning per subscriber, multiply the annual rate by the per-subscriber invested amount, then divide by 12 (to turn an annual figure into a monthly one).

Org monthly share per sub = annual rate x invested-per-sub / 12

Year 1: 0.135 x 1,651.72 / 12 = 18.58
Year 2: 0.155 x 1,882.30 / 12 = 24.31

The organization earns about 18.58 per subscriber per month in Year 1, 24.31 in Year 2.

Line 3: Cost of the customer's monthly profit (per subscriber)

Plain English. This is what it costs the organization to pay the customer their monthly profit. The customer is promised a return at an annual rate (9% in Year 1), so per subscriber per month it is the annual rate times invested-per-sub divided by 12. This is money leaving the organization toward the customer.

Cost of customer monthly profit per sub = annual rate x invested-per-sub / 12

Year 1: 0.09 x 1,651.72 / 12 = 12.39
Year 2: 0.11 x 1,882.30 / 12 = 17.25

It costs about 12.39 per subscriber per month in Year 1, 17.25 in Year 2, to pay the customer's profit.

The spread (why the primary leg is profitable). The organization earns 18.58 per subscriber (Line 2) but only pays out 12.39 of that as the customer's profit (Line 3) in Year 1. The difference, about 6.19 per subscriber, is the organization's gross margin on the re-investment leg before other legs and costs. This spread between what the money earns and what the customer is paid is the heart of the model.

Line 4: Customer's monthly revenue from other legs

Plain English. Beyond the invested-amount profit, the customer also earns from "other legs" (the secondary income from partnerships and innovations), set at 11% of the invested-per-sub amount. Note this rate is applied directly (not divided by 12 in the sheet).

Customer monthly rev from other legs = 11% x invested-per-sub

Year 1: 0.11 x 1,651.72 = 181.69
Year 2: 0.11 x 1,882.30 = 207.05

Line 5: Minus platform usage charges (keep 85%)

Plain English. The platform takes a 15% usage charge on the other-legs revenue, so the customer keeps 85% of it. Multiply Line 4 by 0.85.

Other legs after charges = Line 4 x 0.85

Year 1: 181.69 x 0.85 = 154.44
Year 2: 207.05 x 0.85 = 176.00

Line 6: What the customer earns (total)

Plain English. The customer's total monthly earning is the other-legs revenue after charges (Line 5) plus the customer's monthly profit from the invested amount (Line 3, which from the customer's side is income).

What customer earns = (other legs after charges) + (customer monthly profit)

Year 1: 154.44 + 12.39 = 166.82
Year 2: 176.00 + 17.25 = 193.25

Year-on-year change = (193.25 / 166.82) - 1
                    = 0.1584 = +15.84%

The customer earns about 166.82 per month in Year 1, growing 15.84% to 193.25 in Year 2.

Line 7: What the organization earns (after monthly costs)

Plain English. The organization's total monthly earning combines its margin on both legs, scaled to the whole cohort, then reduced by monthly running costs. The margin per subscriber is: (org re-investment share minus customer profit cost) plus (other-legs revenue minus the part the customer keeps). Multiply by the cohort, then keep 95% in Year 1 (5% monthly costs) and 98% in Year 2 (2% monthly costs).

Org earns = [ (Line2 - Line3) + (Line4 - Line5) ] x Cohort x (1 - monthly cost rate)

Year 1 per-sub margin = (18.58 - 12.39) + (181.69 - 154.44)
                      =  6.19 + 27.25 = 33.45 (approx)
Year 1 = 33.45 x 34,493 x 0.95  =  1,096,014

Year 2 per-sub margin = (24.31 - 17.25) + (207.05 - 176.00)
                      =  7.06 + 31.05 = 38.11 (approx)
Year 2 = 38.11 x 34,493 x 0.98  =  1,288,460

Year-on-year change = (1,288,460 / 1,096,014) - 1
                    = 0.1756 = +17.56%

The organization earns about 1,096,014 per month in Year 1 and 1,288,460 in Year 2 (the 0.95 and 0.98 multipliers apply the 5% and 2% monthly costs).

Line 8: Shareholder share, and the organization's FINAL earnings

Plain English. Shareholders (the owners who funded the company) take a cut: 18% in Year 1, 21% in Year 2. The organization's final earnings are what is left after paying the shareholders.

Shareholder amount = Org earns x shareholder %
Final earnings    = Org earns - shareholder amount

Year 1: shareholder = 1,096,014 x 0.18 = 197,283
        final       = 1,096,014 - 197,283 = 898,732

Year 2: shareholder = 1,288,460 x 0.21 = 270,577
        final       = 1,288,460 - 270,577 = 1,017,883

Year-on-year change in final = (1,017,883 / 898,732) - 1
                             = 0.1326 = +13.26%

The organization's final monthly earnings are about 898,732 in Year 1 and 1,017,883 in Year 2.

Exam trap (the Basic to Advance rule of thumb). The worksheet states: as you move up from Basic to Intermediate to Advance, the number of customers shrinks by roughly half at each step, and the organization-versus-customer margin trade-off also shifts by some percent. So Advance has far fewer subscribers than Basic, but typically a different (often richer) per-subscriber margin. Do not assume the cohort size stays constant across variants; it roughly halves each step up.

What Wage Boost is

Wage Boost is a mobile app that empowers daily wage earners by combining micro-lending (small, easy loans for immediate needs) with short-term investment opportunities that give quick returns. Its purpose is to offer quick-return investments, enhance financial inclusion, and provide value-added services for daily sustenance and growth, all through tech-enabled products and partnerships.

It follows the 9-part structure exactly:

  • Background & hypothesis: daily wage earners face financial challenges due to limited access to traditional banking; the team hypothesizes significant demand for micro-lending and short-term investments with quick returns, and an opportunity to fill the gap with low-entry-barrier products (minimal investment requirements).
  • Problem statement: daily earners face financial exclusion, traditional products are not tailored to low-income workers, and high interest rates plus a lack of flexible short-term options block economic mobility. Wage Boost provides an app-based platform for accessible micro-lending and investment designed for daily earners.
  • Assumptions: users have smartphones and reliable internet; a minimum investment threshold is achievable and acceptable; users are interested in and can understand short-term investing and lending; and quick returns are a primary incentive driving regular engagement.
  • Services: micro-lending with a simplified approval process; short-term investments with quick returns; partnership deals with mobile companies and food chains (installment plans, delivery-job options); educational resources for financial literacy; and an ecosystem for small-business investments.
  • Target audience and segments: primarily daily wage earners needing immediate help and investment; the underserved (small traders, street vendors); gig-economy workers needing flexible solutions; and small business owners wanting low-barrier investment to grow.
  • Value Proposition Canvas (customer journey): reduces financial stress with quick micro-lending and investment returns; saves time by removing physical banking visits and paperwork; provides a seamless, user-friendly interface even for non-tech-savvy users; and creates opportunities for new economic activity and community growth.
  • Financials: per-cohort profitability tables (same structure as the lab model) for each variant.
  • Scale-up plan: a plan to grow the user base over time.

Wage Boost's acquisition strategy: ATL and BTL

Wage Boost splits its upsell and acquisition into two classic marketing categories:

  • Above-the-Line (ATL), to create app awareness: broad mass-media tactics. Wage Boost uses TikTok (primary mass platform), digital advertising via in-app pop-up notifications, and promotional text (SMS) messages. ATL casts a wide net to build awareness.
  • Below-the-Line (BTL), distribution channels and partnerships: targeted, direct tactics. Wage Boost uses retailers and growth partners, and a collaboration with Akhuwat (a well-known interest-free microfinance organization in Pakistan). BTL reaches specific audiences through trusted intermediaries.

Key term. ATL vs BTL. Above-the-Line is mass, untargeted advertising for broad awareness (TV, radio, mass social media). Below-the-Line is targeted, direct activity aimed at specific customers (retail partnerships, direct distribution, on-ground collaboration). Wage Boost's Akhuwat partnership is a smart BTL move because Akhuwat already has trust and reach among exactly the low-income segment Wage Boost targets.

Wage Boost shows the expected depth: a clear hypothesis and problem statement, explicit assumptions, well-defined variants with distinct eligibility, a services list tied to benefits, named target segments, a value proposition canvas describing the customer journey, per-cohort financials, a scale-up plan, and a concrete acquisition plan split into ATL and BTL. It even states what it did not cover (long-term investment strategies, insurance, broader savings) and how it could improve (more personalization, more user education), which is exactly the kind of honest scoping a strong submission includes.

AI Paradigms stack (highest priority)

  • Applied AI = Expert Systems (Enforcer: rules, certainty, compliance, risk = brittle) + Machine Learning (Predictor: probabilities, churn/credit/NBA, risk = degrades) + Deep Learning (Pattern Finder: fraud/images, risk = opaque).
  • Generative AI (Creator): makes new content, risk = hallucination.
  • Agentic AI (Orchestrator): plans and acts autonomously, risk = automates mistakes, needs guardrails.
  • The line: fit-for-purpose, not one-size-fits-all; GenAI expands the toolbox, it does not replace ML or Applied AI.
  • GIGO: AI amplifies dysfunction; it rewards data integrity, not intention. Autonomy without control == Risk.

CVM and growth

  • CVM = grow the lifetime value of existing customers. Keep investing while CAC < CLV; CAC > CLV is "Start-up Demise". k = tenure.
  • 5 imperatives: mobile mind shift, big data to insights, transform CX, digital disruptor, embrace AI.
  • Data Mix: WHO = demographics, WHEN = in-market timing (3rd-party cookie decline), WHAT = transactions, WHY = surveys.
  • Growth types: Promotional (short-term) vs Emotional (durable loyalty). Sustainable growth needs more emotional.
  • Segmentation: macro (demographics) / strategic (long-term) / micro (CLV, RFM, IBRO, cohort, ML-DL unsupervised). ATL = mass, BTL = targeted.

Experiments and hypothesis

  • Target vs Control = communication effect (stratified sampling, apple to apple).
  • A/B test = best message (champion vs challenger).
  • Model Performance = modelling effect (random vs high-propensity).
  • Total value = doing nothing vs running all three.
  • Null = no change; Alternative = change. Directional (one-tailed) = uplift only; Non-directional (two-tailed) = up or down. Decide only with statistical significance.

Digital products

  • Products = tangible; Services = intangible. The market rewards relevance.
  • MVP/Basic = minimum for the core benefit; Augmented = beyond minimum expectations.
  • 4 types: Digital Goods, Retail/Distribution, Augmented Products, Digital Services.
  • Two I's: Interactivity (it reacts, gathers data) and Individualization (personalized prices/promotions).
  • 8 steps: Idea Generation, Idea Screening, Concept Development and Testing, Marketing Strategy and Business Analysis, Prototype, Iterative Testing, Commercialization, Breakeven. First-mover and fast-follower advantage; S-curve lifecycle.

Pricing (9 strategies)

  • Cost-Plus, Competitive (red-ocean risk), Penetration (low then raise: Netflix, Uber), Dynamic (surge: airlines, hotels), Price Discrimination (location-based), Economy (Walmart, IKEA, Southwest), Premium (Apple, Rolex), Loss Leader (Kindle, Gillette), Value-Based (the holy grail).
  • Digital nuance: near-zero marginal cost, so cost-plus and dynamic are less relevant; value-based wins.
  • Pricing Game: Revenue = Customers x Subscription Amount x Pricing rate. Orange = 5% subscription cut (max 2 segments). Target = +20% revenue. The 5% cut lowers revenue, so the price rise must over-compensate.

NBA and the Base Exclusion Funnel

  • NBA = right offer, right customer, right time, right channel. Four tiers: Understand customer, Decide offer, Place/Pitch on channel, Adaptive learning and self-healing. Dormancy 0 to 150 days. Results: 2 to 18 products, activations 11% to 19%.
  • Funnel: Combined Score 0 to 400; mu = 170.85, sigma = 103.12, range 1 to 368. mu +/- 2 sigma = [-35, 377] is wider than the data, so it excludes nobody; practical limits near 20 and 350.
  • Flags: Qualify (inside limits), Slide back (top 3 model deciles), Retained (Qualify or Slide back), Final (Retained and not fraud).
